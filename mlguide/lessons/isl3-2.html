Real responses depend on many things at once. Multiple linear regression gives every predictor its own slope in one model, and in doing so it can reverse a conclusion that a one-predictor regression got wrong. This lesson shows you how the coefficients are computed with matrices, how to test whether any predictor matters at all, and how to put honest error bars on a prediction.

You'll use ISLP 3.1 (least squares, SE, t, R²) · The design matrix · Matrix multiplication · Solving \(\mathbf{A}\bx=\mathbf{b}\) · Least squares as projection · The danger of many tests

Instead of fitting a separate simple linear regression model for each predictor, a better approach is to extend the simple linear regression model (3.5) so that it can directly accommodate multiple predictors.

The multiple linear regression model

The fix: give every predictor its own slope, all in one model. With \(p\) predictors,

Multiple linear regression (3.19)
\[Y=\beta_0+\beta_1X_1+\beta_2X_2+\dots+\beta_pX_p+\epsilon\]

Read: “Y equals beta-nought plus beta-one X-one plus beta-two X-two, and so on up to beta-p X-p, plus epsilon.”

SymbolSay it as / meaningAdvertising example
\(p\)“p”: the number of predictors\(p=3\)
\(X_j\)“X-sub-j”: the \(j\)-th predictor\(X_1=\) TV, \(X_2=\) radio, \(X_3=\) newspaper
\(\beta_j\)“beta-j”: the average change in \(Y\) for a one-unit increase in \(X_j\), holding all other predictors fixed\(\beta_2\): extra sales per $1,000 of radio at fixed TV and newspaper
\(x_{ij}\)“x-i-j”: the value of predictor \(j\) for observation \(i\)\(x_{1,2}=37.8\): market 1's radio budget

For Advertising this becomes

The Advertising model (3.20)
\[\text{sales}=\beta_0+\beta_1\times\text{TV}+\beta_2\times\text{radio}+\beta_3\times\text{newspaper}+\epsilon\]

The phrase “holding all other predictors fixed” is the key to the whole lesson. In a simple regression, the newspaper slope answers: “in markets that spend more on newspapers, how much higher are sales?” In the multiple regression it answers a different, sharper question: “among markets with the same TV and radio budgets, how much higher are sales where newspaper spending is higher?”

Estimating the coefficients: least squares again

Given estimates \(\hat\beta_0,\dots,\hat\beta_p\), predictions are

Prediction (3.21)
\[\hat y=\hat\beta_0+\hat\beta_1x_1+\hat\beta_2x_2+\dots+\hat\beta_px_p\]

and we choose the coefficients exactly as before: to minimise the residual sum of squares,

RSS for multiple regression (3.22)
\[\RSS=\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\hat y_i)^2=\sum_{i=1}^n\big(y_i-\hat\beta_0-\hat\beta_1x_{i1}-\hat\beta_2x_{i2}-\dots-\hat\beta_px_{ip}\big)^2\]

With two predictors, the fitted “line” becomes a flat plane in 3-D, and the residuals are vertical sticks from each point to the plane.

The book says the multiple-regression formulas are “most easily represented using matrix algebra” and leaves them out. You learnt the matrix tools in Vectors and matrices, so we can write them down. Here they are.

The design matrix

Stack the data into a matrix \(\bX\) with one row per observation and one column per coefficient. The first column is all 1s: it multiplies \(\beta_0\). (This is what sm.add_constant did in lesson 3.1.) Stack the responses into a column vector \(\by\) and the coefficients into \(\bbeta\):

\[\bX=\begin{pmatrix}1&x_{11}&x_{12}&\cdots&x_{1p}\\1&x_{21}&x_{22}&\cdots&x_{2p}\\\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\1&x_{n1}&x_{n2}&\cdots&x_{np}\end{pmatrix},\qquad \by=\begin{pmatrix}y_1\\y_2\\\vdots\\y_n\end{pmatrix},\qquad \bbeta=\begin{pmatrix}\beta_0\\\beta_1\\\vdots\\\beta_p\end{pmatrix}.\]

Then all \(n\) predictions at once are the matrix–vector product \(\hat\by=\bX\hat\bbeta\) (row \(i\) of \(\bX\) dotted with \(\hat\bbeta\) gives \(\hat y_i\)), and \(\RSS=\|\by-\bX\hat\bbeta\|^2\), the squared length of the residual vector.

The normal equations

Setting the gradient of RSS to zero (one partial derivative per coefficient, exactly as in the derivation of lesson 3.1, but \(p+1\) of them) gives a set of linear equations called the normal equations:

Normal equations and the least-squares solution
\[\bX\T\bX\,\hat\bbeta=\bX\T\by\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\hat\bbeta=(\bX\T\bX)^{-1}\bX\T\by\]

Read: “X-transpose X times beta-hat equals X-transpose y.” \(\bX\T\bX\) is a small \((p+1)\times(p+1)\) matrix; \(\bX\T\by\) is a vector of length \(p+1\). Solve the system and you have every coefficient.

Why “normal”? Because the equations say \(\bX\T(\by-\bX\hat\bbeta)=\mathbf{0}\): the residual vector is perpendicular (normal) to every column of \(\bX\). Geometrically, \(\hat\by\) is the shadow (projection) of \(\by\) onto the space spanned by the columns; see least squares as a shadow. With one predictor the first row of this system is exactly \(\sum e_i=0\) and the second is \(\sum x_ie_i=0\), the two facts we checked by hand in lesson 3.1.

\(\hat\bbeta=(1,\ 2,\ 3)\): \(\hat y=1+2x_1+3x_2\), RSS = 0

Now add a fourth market, \((x_1,x_2,y)=(1,1,5)\). The plane above would predict \(1+2+3=6\), so this point sits 1 below it, and no plane can pass through all four points any more. The code below redoes the computation with NumPy.

After adding the fourth point \((1,1,5)\), what happens to the fitted coefficients?

  1. They stay \((1,2,3)\); the new point just has a residual of −1
  2. They all change a lot
  3. The slopes stay 2 and 3 but the intercept drops a little, so all four points share the miss
Least squares spreads the miss over all points. Here the new point is “average” in both \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) (they equal their means of 1), so moving the plane straight down is the cheapest fix: the intercept falls from 1 to 0.75, the slopes are unchanged, and the residuals become 0.25, 0.25, 0.25 and −0.75. Option A would leave RSS = 1; option C gives RSS = 0.75, which is smaller.
X3 = np.array([[1, 0, 1],
               [1, 1, 0],
               [1, 2, 2]], dtype=float)
y3 = np.array([4, 3, 11], dtype=float)
print("X^T X =\n", X3.T @ X3)
print("X^T y =", X3.T @ y3)
print("beta_hat (3 points):", np.linalg.solve(X3.T @ X3, X3.T @ y3))

X4 = np.vstack([X3, [1, 1, 1]])          # add a 4th row
y4 = np.append(y3, 5)
b4 = np.linalg.solve(X4.T @ X4, X4.T @ y4)
print("beta_hat (4 points):", b4)
print("residuals:", y4 - X4 @ b4, " RSS =", np.sum((y4 - X4 @ b4) ** 2))
Read the output: the same \(\bX\T\bX\), \(\bX\T\by\) and \((1,2,3)\) as on paper, then \((0.75, 2, 3)\) with four points and RSS 0.75. @ is matrix multiplication and .T the transpose. np.linalg.solve(A, b) solves \(\mathbf{A}\bx=\mathbf{b}\) directly; it is faster and more accurate than computing the inverse with np.linalg.inv and multiplying. np.vstack stacks a new row under a matrix.

Same maths, different name. In signal processing, \(\bX\T\bX\hat\bbeta=\bX\T\by\) is the matrix form of the Wiener–Hopf equations: \(\bX\T\bX\) plays the autocorrelation matrix \(\mathbf{R}\), and \(\bX\T\by\) the cross-correlation vector \(\mathbf{p}\). A least-squares FIR filter design solves exactly this system.

The correlation between radio and newspaper is 0.35: markets that spend a lot on newspapers tend also to spend a lot on radio. Now follow the chain:

  1. Radio really does boost sales.
  2. Markets with big radio budgets also tend to have big newspaper budgets.
  3. So markets with big newspaper budgets tend to have high sales, because they also have big radio budgets.

A simple regression of sales on newspaper sees step 3 and credits newspaper. Newspaper is acting as a surrogate for radio: it “gets credit” for radio's effect. The multiple regression compares markets with the same radio budget, and then the newspaper effect disappears.

The book's absurd example. At a beach town, ice-cream sales and shark attacks rise and fall together, so a regression of shark attacks on ice-cream sales shows a positive slope. Nobody proposes banning ice cream. Hot weather drives both: more people go to the beach, so more ice cream is sold and more people swim with sharks. Add temperature to the regression and ice-cream sales stop being significant. Temperature is to ice cream what radio is to newspaper.

Read the output: Table 3.5. .corr() computes the correlation between every pair of columns. (Our TV–newspaper value prints as 0.0566; the book rounds it to 0.0567.)
These are simulated markets where newspaper has no effect at all (true slope 0) and radio does. Start at correlation 0.35, like the real data: the red simple-regression slope is clearly positive, while the blue dashed multiple-regression slope is close to 0. Slide the correlation to 0: the red slope collapses too, because newspaper no longer “borrows” radio's effect. Slide it to 0.9: the red slope grows, but the blue one stays near the truth.

Pitfall: the multiple regression coefficient answers a different question. Neither table is “wrong”. The simple slope answers “how do sales differ between markets with different newspaper budgets?”, and the answer really is “more newspaper, more sales”. The multiple slope answers “what is newspaper's association with sales after accounting for TV and radio?”. For deciding where to spend money, you want the second.

Question one: is there any relationship at all? The F-statistic

The book lists four questions for any multiple regression:

  1. Is at least one of the predictors \(X_1,\dots,X_p\) useful in predicting the response?
  2. Do all the predictors help to explain \(Y\), or only a subset?
  3. How well does the model fit the data?
  4. Given predictor values, what should we predict, and how accurate is that prediction?

For question 1, the null hypothesis is that every slope is zero:

\[H_0:\ \beta_1=\beta_2=\dots=\beta_p=0\qquad\text{versus}\qquad H_a:\ \text{at least one }\beta_j\text{ is non-zero.}\]

The test uses the F-statistic:

F-statistic (3.23)
\[F=\frac{(\text{TSS}-\RSS)/p}{\RSS/(n-p-1)}\]

Read: “F equals TSS minus RSS over p, divided by RSS over n minus p minus 1.” Top: the variance explained, per predictor. Bottom: the leftover variance, per degree of freedom (this is \(\text{RSE}^2\)).

Why does this work? If the model assumptions hold, the bottom always averages to \(\sigma^2\): \(\E\{\RSS/(n-p-1)\}=\sigma^2\). If \(H_0\) is true, the top also averages to \(\sigma^2\): \(\E\{(\text{TSS}-\RSS)/p\}=\sigma^2\), because with no real predictors the “explained” part is just noise that the fit happened to soak up. So under \(H_0\), \(F\) is near 1. If \(H_a\) is true, the top is bigger than \(\sigma^2\) and \(F\) is greater than 1.

Worked example · the F-statistic for sales on TV, radio and newspaper
\(n=200\), \(p=3\), \(\text{TSS}=5417.15\), \(\RSS=556.83\) (from the fit above).
  1. Explained sum of squares. \(\text{TSS}-\RSS=5417.15-556.83=4860.32\).
  2. Per predictor. \(4860.32/3=1620.11\).
  3. Leftover per degree of freedom. \(n-p-1=200-3-1=196\). \(556.83/196=2.8410\). (Its square root, 1.686, is the RSE.)
  4. Divide. \(F=1620.11/2.8410=570.3\).
  5. Interpret. If no medium mattered, we would expect \(F\approx1\). We got 570. The p-value from the F-distribution with \((3,\,196)\) degrees of freedom is about \(10^{-96}\): overwhelming evidence that at least one medium is associated with sales.

How big must F be? It depends on \(n\) and \(p\). With large \(n\), an F only a little above 1 can already be strong evidence; with small \(n\), a larger F is needed. When the errors are normal (or \(n\) is large), F follows an F-distribution under \(H_0\), and software turns it into a p-value as above.

Testing a subset of coefficients

Sometimes we want to test only some of the coefficients, say the last \(q\): \(H_0:\beta_{p-q+1}=\dots=\beta_p=0\). Fit a second, smaller model without those \(q\) variables and call its residual sum of squares \(\RSS_0\). Then

F-statistic for \(q\) coefficients (3.24)
\[F=\frac{(\RSS_0-\RSS)/q}{\RSS/(n-p-1)}\]

Read: “how much RSS goes up when we drop the q variables, per variable, relative to the leftover variance of the big model.”

Example: drop newspaper (\(q=1\)). The TV + radio model has \(\RSS_0=556.91\); the full model \(\RSS=556.83\). \(F=(556.91-556.83)/1\,/\,2.8410=0.0887/2.8410=0.031\). And the newspaper t-statistic in Table 3.4 was \(-0.1767\): \((-0.1767)^2=0.031\). That is no coincidence: each t-test in Table 3.4 is exactly the F-test for dropping that one variable, and \(t^2=F\). It measures the partial effect of adding that variable to a model that already has the others.

Why look at F when we already have the individual p-values?

It seems that if any individual p-value is small, at least one predictor must matter. This logic fails when \(p\) is large. Suppose \(p=100\) and \(H_0\) is true: no predictor matters at all. Each individual test still has a 5% chance of a p-value below 0.05 by luck, so we expect about \(100\times0.05=5\) small p-values, and the chance of at least one is \(1-0.95^{100}\approx0.994\). (You saw this in the danger of many tests.) The F-statistic adjusts for the number of predictors: if \(H_0\) is true, there is only a 5% chance its p-value falls below 0.05, however many predictors there are.

The F-test needs \(p\) to be small compared with \(n\). If \(p>n\), there are more coefficients than observations, least squares cannot even be fitted, and neither F nor most of this chapter applies. That high-dimensional setting is the subject of Chapter 6.

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
n_sim, p_sim = 200, 100
Xn = rng.normal(size=(n_sim, p_sim))     # 100 predictors of pure noise
yn = rng.normal(size=n_sim)              # a response unrelated to all of them
res_noise = sm.OLS(yn, sm.add_constant(Xn)).fit()
print("individual p-values below 0.05:", np.sum(res_noise.pvalues[1:] < 0.05))
print("overall F p-value:", round(res_noise.f_pvalue, 3))
Read the output: several of the 100 useless predictors look “significant” on their own (the exact count depends on the seed; around 5 is typical), yet the overall F-test correctly finds no evidence of any relationship. res_noise.pvalues[1:] skips the intercept's p-value.

Question two: which variables matter? Variable selection

Once F says “at least one predictor matters”, we want to know which. Choosing the subset of predictors to keep is called variable selection. Chapter 6 treats it properly; here is the classical outline.

Ideally we would fit every possible model and pick the best by a criterion that rewards fit but penalises size: Mallow's \(C_p\), the Akaike information criterion (AIC), the Bayesian information criterion (BIC) or adjusted \(R^2\) (all in Chapter 6). But with \(p\) predictors there are \(2^p\) subsets (each predictor is either in or out). For \(p=2\) that is 4 models; for \(p=30\) it is \(2^{30}=1{,}073{,}741{,}824\). Too many. So we use greedy shortcuts:

MethodHow it worksNotes
Forward selectionStart with the null model (intercept only). Try adding each remaining variable; keep the one giving the lowest RSS. Repeat until a stopping rule says stop.Can always be used, even if \(p>n\). Greedy: may add a variable early that later becomes redundant.
Backward selectionStart with all variables. Remove the one with the largest p-value (least significant). Refit and repeat until, say, all remaining p-values are below a threshold.Cannot be used if \(p>n\) (the full model cannot be fitted).
Mixed selectionStart like forward selection, adding the best variable each step. But if a variable already in the model rises above a p-value threshold (which can happen as others are added), remove it. Continue until everything in the model is significant and nothing outside would be.Repairs forward selection's greediness.

def rss_of(cols):

Question three: how well does the model fit?

We use the same two measures as in lesson 3.1: RSE and \(R^2\). In multiple regression, \(R^2\) equals \(\text{Cor}(Y,\hat Y)^2\), the squared correlation between the response and the fitted values. In fact the least-squares fit maximises this correlation among all linear models.

Two comparisons from the book:

  • All three media: \(R^2=0.8972\). TV and radio only: \(R^2=0.89719\). Adding newspaper raises \(R^2\) by a hair. \(R^2\) always increases when you add a variable, even a useless one, because adding a column can never increase the training RSS (the old fit is still available with the new coefficient set to 0). A tiny increase is extra evidence that newspaper can be dropped; keeping it would likely hurt on new data by overfitting.
  • TV only: \(R^2=0.61\). Adding radio jumps to 0.897: a substantial improvement.

For RSE, the general formula has \(n-p-1\) in the denominator:

Residual standard error, \(p\) predictors (3.25)
\[\text{RSE}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n-p-1}\RSS}\]

With \(p=1\) this is the \(\sqrt{\RSS/(n-2)}\) of lesson 3.1.

This explains a puzzle: TV + radio has RSE 1.681, but TV + radio + newspaper has RSE 1.686, higher, even though its RSS is lower. Adding newspaper cut RSS by only 0.09 while the denominator fell from 197 to 196: \(\sqrt{556.91/197}=1.6814\) versus \(\sqrt{556.83/196}=1.6855\). Models with more variables can have higher RSE when the RSS drop is small relative to the lost degree of freedom.

Question four: predictions and their uncertainty

Prediction itself is easy: plug the budgets into (3.21). But how accurate is it? Three sources of uncertainty:

  1. The coefficients are estimates. The least-squares plane \(\hat Y=\hat\beta_0+\hat\beta_1X_1+\dots+\hat\beta_pX_p\) only estimates the population plane \(f(X)=\beta_0+\beta_1X_1+\dots+\beta_pX_p\). This is the reducible error of Chapter 2. A confidence interval tells us how close \(\hat Y\) is likely to be to \(f(X)\).
  2. Model bias. The true \(f\) is probably not exactly linear, so we are really estimating the best linear approximation. The book sets this aside here.
  3. The irreducible error \(\epsilon\). Even with the true coefficients, one market's sales vary around \(f(X)\). A prediction interval covers this too, so it is always wider.

Which interval? Use a confidence interval for the average sales over many cities with these budgets. Use a prediction interval for the sales of one particular city. The book's example, for $100,000 on TV and $20,000 on radio (TV + radio model): prediction 11,256 units; 95% confidence interval [10,985, 11,528]; 95% prediction interval [7,930, 14,580]. Same centre, very different widths.

For simple regression the formulas are worth seeing (multiple regression uses the matrix version, which software handles). At a new point \(x_0\), with \(\hat y_0=\hat\beta_0+\hat\beta_1x_0\):

Intervals at \(x_0\) (simple regression)
\[\text{CI: }\ \hat y_0\pm t_{0.975,n-2}\,\text{RSE}\sqrt{\frac1n+\frac{(x_0-\bar x)^2}{S_{xx}}},\qquad \text{PI: }\ \hat y_0\pm t_{0.975,n-2}\,\text{RSE}\sqrt{1+\frac1n+\frac{(x_0-\bar x)^2}{S_{xx}}}\]

The prediction interval has an extra “1 +” under the root: that is the variance of the new observation's own \(\epsilon\), measured in units of \(\sigma^2\).

The intervals are narrowest at \(x_0=\bar x\) and widen as you move away, because a small error in the slope matters more far from the centre of the data.

Worked example · the book's intervals at TV = 100, radio = 20
TV + radio model: \(\hat y_0=2.9211+0.04575\times100+0.18799\times20\). From statsmodels: standard error of the mean prediction \(\SE(\hat y_0)=0.1375\); RSE \(=1.6814\); \(n-p-1=197\), \(t_{0.975,197}=1.9721\).
  1. Prediction. \(2.9211+4.5754+3.7598=11.2565\) thousand units: 11,256.
  2. Confidence interval. Half-width \(1.9721\times0.1375=0.2712\). Interval \([11.2565-0.2712,\ 11.2565+0.2712]=[10.985,\ 11.528]\).
  3. Prediction interval. The standard error for one new market combines both sources: \(\sqrt{\text{RSE}^2+\SE(\hat y_0)^2}=\sqrt{2.8271+0.0189}=\sqrt{2.8460}=1.6870\). Half-width \(1.9721\times1.6870=3.3269\). Interval \([7.930,\ 14.583]\).
  4. In words. Over many cities spending these amounts, average sales are between about 10,985 and 11,528 units. A single such city will sell between about 7,930 and 14,580 units, 95% of the time.

Interview classic: “What is the difference between a confidence interval and a prediction interval for a regression?” The confidence interval is for the mean response \(f(x_0)\) and reflects only uncertainty in the coefficients; it shrinks to zero width as \(n\to\infty\). The prediction interval is for a single new \(y_0\) and adds the irreducible noise \(\sigma^2\), so it never shrinks below about \(\pm2\sigma\).

This is one greedy step: try each candidate, keep the best. np.inf (infinity) is a starting value that any real RSS beats. Calling it repeatedly, moving the winner from candidates to chosen, is forward selection.

Quiz

In the multiple regression of sales on TV, radio and newspaper, \(\hat\beta_{\text{radio}}=0.189\). What does it mean?

  1. Radio causes 189 extra units per $1,000
  2. An extra $1,000 on radio is associated with about 189 more units, holding TV and newspaper fixed
  3. Radio explains 18.9% of the variance in sales
Multiple regression coefficients are partial effects “holding the others fixed”. Regression on observational data shows association, not causation. 0.189 is a slope, not a share of variance.

Newspaper is significant in a simple regression (p = 0.001) but not in the multiple regression (p = 0.86). The best explanation is:

  1. The multiple regression has a coding error
  2. Newspaper has a negative effect that cancels a positive one
  3. Newspaper is correlated with radio (0.35), so alone it picks up radio's effect
This is the surrogate effect: markets that spend on newspapers also spend on radio. Once radio is held fixed, newspaper adds nothing.

A regression has \(n=24\), \(p=3\), TSS = 100 and RSS = 40. Compute the F-statistic.

Top: \((\text{TSS}-\RSS)/p=(100-40)/3=60/3=20\). Bottom: \(\RSS/(n-p-1)=40/(24-3-1)=40/20=2\). \(F=20/2=10\).

The full Advertising model has RSS = 556.83 with \(n=200\) and \(p=3\). Compute its RSE (3 decimals).

\(\text{RSE}=\sqrt{556.83/(200-3-1)}=\sqrt{556.83/196}=\sqrt{2.8410}=1.686\). Dividing by 197 or 200 instead of 196 is the usual slip.

You add a column of pure random noise to a regression. What happens to the training \(R^2\)?

  1. It increases slightly (or stays the same)
  2. It decreases
  3. It can go either way
Adding a column can never increase the training RSS (setting its coefficient to zero recovers the old fit), so \(R^2=1-\RSS/\text{TSS}\) cannot fall. This is why \(R^2\) alone cannot choose between models of different sizes.

With \(p=100\) useless predictors, why is looking for any individual p-value below 0.05 a bad test of “is anything related?”

  1. Individual p-values cannot be computed when \(p\) is large
  2. About 5 of them will fall below 0.05 by chance alone, so we would almost always wrongly say yes
  3. The p-values would all be exactly 1
Each test has a 5% false-alarm rate, so with 100 tests about 5 false alarms are expected, and at least one is nearly certain. The F-test controls the overall false-alarm rate at 5%.

You want a range for the sales of one specific new city with given budgets. Which interval?

  1. Confidence interval for the coefficients
  2. Confidence interval for the mean response
  3. Prediction interval
One city's sales include its own random error \(\epsilon\), so you need the wider prediction interval. The confidence interval for the mean covers the average over many such cities.

Which variable-selection method cannot be used when \(p>n\)?

  1. Backward selection
  2. Forward selection
  3. Both can always be used
Backward selection starts by fitting the model with all \(p\) predictors, which is impossible by least squares when there are more coefficients than observations. Forward selection starts from the intercept-only model and can stop before reaching \(n\) variables.

Explain in your own words why the normal equations are called “normal”, and what the first equation says about the residuals.

They can be written \(\bX\T(\by-\bX\hat\bbeta)=\mathbf{0}\): the residual vector \(\mathbf{e}=\by-\hat\by\) has zero dot product with every column of \(\bX\), so it is perpendicular (“normal”) to all of them; \(\hat\by\) is the projection of \(\by\) onto the column space. The first column of \(\bX\) is all 1s, so the first equation says \(\sum_i e_i=0\): the residuals add to zero.
