Why formulas look hard, and why they are not

A formula packs a long sentence into a few symbols. Take this one from ISLP Chapter 3:

An example sentence in maths
\[\RSS=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(y_i-\hat y_i\right)^2\]

Read: “R-S-S equals the sum, for \(i\) from 1 to \(n\), of \(y\)-sub-\(i\) minus \(y\)-hat-sub-\(i\), all squared.”

In ordinary English that says: for every data point, take the true value, subtract the predicted value, square the difference, then add up all those squares. The English version is 27 words. The maths version is about ten symbols. That compression is the whole reason maths notation exists: it is faster to write, and once you can read it, faster to read.

The difficulty is never the idea. The difficulty is that nobody told you how to say the symbols. When you cannot pronounce something, your brain cannot hold it, so it slides off the page. This lesson fixes that. You will learn the name of every symbol, practise expanding the shorthand back into ordinary arithmetic, and get a strategy for any formula you have never seen.

The one habit that matters most: whenever you meet a formula, put numbers into it. Pick two or three tiny values, write the formula out with those numbers, and compute it. A formula you have computed by hand once is a formula you understand.

You already read compressed maths fluently in your ECE courses. The DFT \(X[k]=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x[n]e^{-j2\pi kn/N}\) is exactly the same kind of sentence as \(\RSS\): an index (\(n\)) that runs over a range, a term that depends on it, and a sum. The only new things in ML are the letters (the books use \(i\) for data points, \(j\) for features) and a few new symbols such as \(\argmin\) and \(\E\).

Data in a table: \(n\), \(p\), \(x_{ij}\), \(\bx_i\) and \(\bX\)

Almost everything in ISLP starts from a table of data. Each row is one observation (one person, one market, one image). Each column is one variable, also called a feature, predictor or input. Here are the first three rows of ISLP's Advertising data: advertising budgets (in thousands of dollars) for TV, radio and newspaper in three markets, and the sales that followed (in thousands of units).

1230.137.869.222.1

317.245.969.39.3

ISLP fixes two letters for the size of any table:

  • \(n\) (read en) is the number of observations, the number of rows. The full Advertising data has \(n=200\) markets.
  • \(p\) (read pee) is the number of predictors, the number of input columns. Here \(p=3\) (TV, radio, newspaper). Sales is the thing we want to predict, so it is not counted in \(p\).

And two letters for which row and column you mean:

  • \(i\) (read eye) always counts rows: \(i=1,2,\dots,n\).
  • \(j\) (read jay) always counts columns: \(j=1,2,\dots,p\).

Written as a real matrix, with round brackets around it, the input table is

\[\bX=\begin{pmatrix}x_{11}&x_{12}&x_{13}\\x_{21}&x_{22}&x_{23}\\x_{31}&x_{32}&x_{33}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}230.1&37.8&69.2\\44.5&39.3&45.1\\17.2&45.9&69.3\end{pmatrix},\qquad \by=\begin{pmatrix}22.1\\10.4\\9.3\end{pmatrix}.\]

Notice how the subscripts of \(x_{ij}\) march across the first matrix: the first subscript stays the same along a row, the second stays the same down a column. In general ISLP writes

\[\bX=\begin{pmatrix}x_{11}&x_{12}&\cdots&x_{1p}\\x_{21}&x_{22}&\cdots&x_{2p}\\\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\x_{n1}&x_{n2}&\cdots&x_{np}\end{pmatrix}.\]

The dots \(\cdots\) (read and so on across), \(\vdots\) (and so on down) and \(\ddots\) (and so on diagonally) mean “the pattern continues”. They save writing all 200 rows.

Row or column? ISLP uses the same letter \(x\) for a row (\(x_i\), indexed by \(i\)) and a column (\(\bx_j\), indexed by \(j\)). Only the letter of the index tells you which one is meant. When you read \(x_3\), ask: is the 3 counting rows or columns? Look at the surrounding sentence. In \(\sum_{i=1}^n x_i\) it counts rows; in \(\beta_1x_1+\beta_2x_2\) it counts columns (predictors).

Transpose: \(\T\)

The little raised \(\T\) (read transpose) turns columns into rows and rows into columns. By convention a vector is a column, so \(x_2\) is a column; when we want it lying flat we write \(x_2\T\) (read x-two transpose):

\[x_2=\begin{pmatrix}44.5\\39.3\\45.1\end{pmatrix},\qquad x_2\T=\begin{pmatrix}44.5&39.3&45.1\end{pmatrix}.\]

For a matrix, the transpose flips it over its main diagonal (top-left to bottom-right): row 1 becomes column 1, row 2 becomes column 2, and so on. For a \(2\times3\) matrix:

\[\begin{pmatrix}{\color{#d97706}\mathbf{1}}&{\color{#d97706}\mathbf{2}}&{\color{#d97706}\mathbf{3}}\\{\color{#2563eb}\mathbf{4}}&{\color{#2563eb}\mathbf{5}}&{\color{#2563eb}\mathbf{6}}\end{pmatrix}\T=\begin{pmatrix}{\color{#d97706}\mathbf{1}}&{\color{#2563eb}\mathbf{4}}\\{\color{#d97706}\mathbf{2}}&{\color{#2563eb}\mathbf{5}}\\{\color{#d97706}\mathbf{3}}&{\color{#2563eb}\mathbf{6}}\end{pmatrix}.\]

The orange row became the orange column, the blue row the blue column. A \(2\times3\) matrix (2 rows, 3 columns) becomes \(3\times2\). You will practise this properly in Vectors and matrices.

Functions: round brackets, square brackets, and parameters

A function is a machine: a value goes in, a value comes out, and the same input always gives the same output. You met this idea in school as \(f(x)=2x+1\): put \(x=3\) in, get \(f(3)=2\cdot3+1=7\) out.

ISLP writes functions the school way, with round brackets: \(f(X)\), \(\hat f(x_0)\), \(p(X)\). Prince writes them with square brackets: \(\mathrm{f}[\bx,\bphi]\), \(\log[x]\), \(\exp[x]\), \(\mathrm{sig}[z]\). The square brackets mean exactly what round brackets mean. Prince's reason is practical: deep-learning formulas contain many round brackets for grouping, like \((a+b)(c-d)\), and square brackets make it instantly clear which brackets hold a function's inputs.

Variables (Roman letters) and parameters (Greek letters)

Prince keeps a strict rule (Appendix A, p. 437): variables are written with Roman (English) letters, \(x, y, z, a, h\); parameters are written with Greek letters, \(\phi, \beta, \omega, \theta\). A variable is something that changes from example to example (each house has a different size \(x\)). A parameter is a setting inside the model that we tune once, during training, and then keep (the slope of the line). ISLP follows the same habit: its coefficients are \(\beta_0,\beta_1,\dots\).

So when Prince writes

A model with parameters (Prince eq. 2.2 and 2.4)
\[y=\mathrm{f}[x,\bphi]=\phi_0+\phi_1x\]

Read: “y equals f of x and phi, which equals phi-nought plus phi-one times x.” The input is \(x\); the parameters are \(\bphi=(\phi_0,\phi_1)\), the intercept and the slope.

the comma inside the brackets separates two kinds of input: the data \(x\) (changes for every example) and the parameters \(\bphi\) (fixed once trained). Bold \(\bphi\) means “all the parameters packed into one vector”.

Worked example · evaluating \(\mathrm{f}[x,\bphi]\)
The model is \(\mathrm{f}[x,\bphi]=\phi_0+\phi_1x\) with parameters \(\bphi=(\phi_0,\phi_1)=(1,\,0.5)\). Evaluate it at the input \(x=4\).
  1. Substitute the parameter values: \(\mathrm{f}[x,\bphi]=1+0.5\,x\).The parameters are fixed numbers once the model is trained.
  2. Substitute the input: \(\mathrm{f}[4,\bphi]=1+0.5\times4\).
  3. Compute: \(1+2=3\).
\(\mathrm{f}[4,\bphi]=3\)

When Prince wants to leave an input deliberately unspecified, he uses a bullet: \(\mathrm{f}[\bullet,\bphi]\) means “the function f, with parameters \(\bphi\), whatever the input”. And when a function returns a vector, its name is bold: \(\mathbf{mlp}[\bx,\bphi]\) returns a vector.

Think of parameters as knobs. \(\mathrm{f}[x,\bphi]=\phi_0+\phi_1x\) is not one line; it is a whole family of lines, one for each setting of the two knobs \(\phi_0\) (where the line crosses the vertical axis) and \(\phi_1\) (how steep it is). Training means turning the knobs until the line fits the data. Prince's Figure 2.1 shows three members of the family.

Minimum and arg-min: the value versus the place

Training a model means finding the parameters that make the loss as small as possible. Prince writes this as

Training as minimisation (Prince eq. 2.3)
\[\hat{\bphi}=\argmin_{\bphi}\Big[L[\bphi]\Big]\]

Read: “phi-hat equals the arg-min over phi of L of phi”: the parameter setting at which the loss \(L\) is smallest. The little \(\bphi\) under argmin says what we are allowed to change.

The difference between \(\min\) and \(\argmin\) confuses almost everyone once, so here it is with a table. Suppose we tried four values of a single parameter \(\phi\) and computed the loss for each:

\(\phi\)0123
\(L[\phi]\)9414

Then \(\min_\phi L[\phi]=1\) (the smallest loss) and \(\argmin_\phi L[\phi]=2\) (the parameter that achieves it). The min is the height of the bottom of the valley; the argmin is where on the map the bottom of the valley is. In ML we almost always want the argmin: we want the parameters, not the loss value.

Sigma notation: the sum sign, with numbers

The capital Greek letter \(\Sigma\) (sigma, the Greek S, for “sum”) is the symbol you will meet most. Every part of it has a job:

The parts of a sum
\[\sum_{\color{#2563eb}i=1}^{\color{#dc2626}n}\ \color{#16a34a}a_i\color{currentColor}\;=\;a_1+a_2+\dots+a_n\]

The blue part names the index (\(i\)) and where it starts (1). The red part says where it stops (\(n\)). The green part is the term: what to compute for each value of the index. Then add all the terms.

To expand a sum, follow a three-step recipe. (1) Write down each value the index takes. (2) For each one, copy the term with the index replaced by that value. (3) Put plus signs between them and add. Let us do it with the data \(x=(x_1,x_2,x_3,x_4)=(2,5,3,6)\), so \(n=4\).

Worked example · five sums of the same four numbers
\(x_1=2,\ x_2=5,\ x_3=3,\ x_4=6\), so \(n=4\).
  1. Plain sum. \(\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{4}x_i=x_1+x_2+x_3+x_4=2+5+3+6=\mathbf{16}.\)
  2. The mean. \(\displaystyle\bar x=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i=\frac{16}{4}=\mathbf{4}.\)The \(\frac1n\) sits outside the sum: add first, then divide once.
  3. Sum of squares. \(\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{4}x_i^2=2^2+5^2+3^2+6^2=4+25+9+36=\mathbf{74}.\)The term is \(x_i^2\): square each value before adding.
  4. Square of the sum. \(\displaystyle\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{4}x_i\Big)^2=16^2=\mathbf{256}.\)Now the square is outside the sum: add first, square once. 256 is not 74. Where the brackets sit changes everything.
  5. Sum of squared deviations from the mean. The deviations \(x_i-\bar x\) are \(2-4=-2,\ 5-4=1,\ 3-4=-1,\ 6-4=2\). Then \[\sum_{i=1}^{4}(x_i-\bar x)^2=(-2)^2+1^2+(-1)^2+2^2=4+1+1+4=\mathbf{10}.\]This quantity is the heart of variance, and of ISLP's \(\TSS\) (total sum of squares) in ISLP 3.1.
\(\sum x_i=16,\quad\bar x=4,\quad\sum x_i^2=74,\quad(\sum x_i)^2=256,\quad\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2=10\)

A few more shapes you will see:

  • A different start and stop. \(\sum_{i=2}^{3}x_i=x_2+x_3=5+3=8\). Only the terms from 2 to 3.
  • The index appears in the term itself. \(\sum_{i=1}^{4}i\,x_i=1\cdot2+2\cdot5+3\cdot3+4\cdot6=2+10+9+24=45\).
  • A term with no index in it. \(\sum_{i=1}^{4}7=7+7+7+7=28\). In general \(\sum_{i=1}^{n}c=nc\): adding the same number \(n\) times.
  • Prince's ranges. Prince often writes \(\sum_{i=1}^{I}\), with a capital \(I\) for the number of examples. Same thing, different letter.
Pick a term and move the start and stop sliders. Every term is written out with its number substituted, and the bars show how each term contributes to the total. Try the term \(x_i^2\) and then \((x_i-\bar x)^2\) with the full range: compare with the worked example. Then set start = stop and see that a sum of one term is just that term.

Three rules for moving things in and out of a sum

You will see derivations in both books that rearrange sums. All of them use only these three rules, each of which is ordinary arithmetic (it is the distributive law and re-ordering of additions):

Sum rules
\[\sum_{i=1}^{n}c\,a_i=c\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i,\qquad \sum_{i=1}^{n}(a_i+b_i)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i+\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_i,\qquad \sum_{i=1}^{n}c=nc\]

A constant multiplier can come out of the sum; a sum of two things splits into two sums; adding a constant \(n\) times gives \(n\) times the constant.

Check the first rule with numbers: \(\sum_{i=1}^4 3x_i=6+15+9+18=48\), and \(3\sum_{i=1}^4x_i=3\times16=48\). Equal. The rule is just “take out the common factor”.

What does not come out of a sum: anything that contains the index. \(\sum_i x_i y_i\ne\big(\sum_i x_i\big)\big(\sum_i y_i\big)\) in general. With \(x=(1,2)\) and \(y=(3,4)\): the left side is \(3+8=11\); the right side is \(3\times7=21\). Squares are the same trap: \(\sum x_i^2\ne(\sum x_i)^2\) (74 versus 256 above).

Double sums: adding over a whole table

When the term has two indices, you will see two sigmas. Read them from the outside in, like two nested loops: for each row \(i\), add along the row over \(j\); then add the row totals. With the table

\[\bX=\begin{pmatrix}1&4&2\\3&0&5\end{pmatrix}\quad(n=2\text{ rows},\ p=3\text{ columns}):\] \[\sum_{i=1}^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{3}x_{ij}=\underbrace{(1+4+2)}_{i=1:\ 7}+\underbrace{(3+0+5)}_{i=2:\ 8}=15.\]

Prince's Frobenius norm of a matrix (Appendix 23.3.2) is a double sum of exactly this kind: square every entry, add them all, take the square root.

Pi notation: products, and why logs turn them into sums

The capital \(\Pi\) (pi, the Greek P, for “product”) works exactly like \(\Sigma\) but multiplies instead of adding. It appears whenever we combine probabilities of independent events, for example the probability of a whole dataset in Prince Chapter 5 (UDL 5):

\[\prod_{i=1}^{3}p_i=p_1\times p_2\times p_3=0.9\times0.8\times0.5=0.36.\]

Products of many small probabilities get tiny very fast (a hundred factors of 0.5 is about \(10^{-30}\)), so the books almost always take the logarithm, which turns the product into a sum, because \(\log(ab)=\log a+\log b\):

\[\log\prod_{i=1}^{3}p_i=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\log p_i=\log0.9+\log0.8+\log0.5=-0.1054-0.2231-0.6931=-1.0217,\]

and indeed \(\log0.36=-1.0217\). This one identity is why “negative log-likelihood” loss functions are sums.

A five-step strategy for reading any formula

When you meet a formula you have never seen, do not try to understand it all at once. Work through these five questions, in order, with a pencil.

  1. What is being computed? Look at the left-hand side. Is it one number, a vector, a function, a probability? Say its name aloud.
  2. What is fixed and what varies? Find every index (\(i\), \(j\), \(k\)) and say what it counts and over what range. Find the parameters (usually Greek) and the data (usually Roman).
  3. Write the first two terms out. Replace the index by 1, then by 2. Most of the mystery disappears once you see \(a_1+a_2+\dots\).
  4. Put in tiny numbers. Two or three data points with small whole numbers. Compute the formula by hand.
  5. Say it in one plain sentence, and ask what would make it big and what would make it small.
Worked example · reading Prince's least-squares loss
Prince eq. 2.5: \(\displaystyle L[\bphi]=\sum_{i=1}^{I}\big(\mathrm{f}[x_i,\bphi]-y_i\big)^2=\sum_{i=1}^{I}\big(\phi_0+\phi_1x_i-y_i\big)^2\). Data: three points \((x_i,y_i)=(1,2),\,(2,3),\,(3,5)\), so \(I=3\). Parameters to try: \(\bphi=(\phi_0,\phi_1)=(1,1)\).
  1. What is computed? \(L[\bphi]\), “L of phi”, the loss: one number that measures how badly the line with parameters \(\bphi\) fits the data.
  2. What varies? The index \(i\) counts data points, from 1 to \(I=3\). \(x_i,y_i\) are data (fixed). \(\phi_0,\phi_1\) are parameters (fixed inside this sum, but they are what training will change).
  3. First two terms. \(L=(\phi_0+\phi_1x_1-y_1)^2+(\phi_0+\phi_1x_2-y_2)^2+\dots\) Each bracket is “prediction minus truth” for one point.
  4. Numbers. With \(\phi_0=1,\phi_1=1\) the predictions are \(1+1\cdot1=2\), \(1+1\cdot2=3\), \(1+1\cdot3=4\). Differences from the truth: \(2-2=0\), \(3-3=0\), \(4-5=-1\). Squares: \(0,\ 0,\ 1\). Sum: \(L=0+0+1=1\).The minus sign of \(-1\) disappears when squared: overshooting and undershooting are punished alike.
  5. One sentence. “The loss adds up the squared vertical gaps between the line and each data point.” It is zero only if the line passes through every point, and big if the line is far from the points.
\(L[(1,1)]=1\)

ISLP writes the same quantity as \(\RSS=\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\hat\beta_0-\hat\beta_1x_i)^2\) (ISLP 3.1). Notice the order inside the bracket is reversed (truth minus prediction), but because the bracket is squared, the answer is identical. Different books, different letters, the same idea.

Worked example · reading ISLP's logistic regression formula
ISLP eq. 4.2: \(\displaystyle p(X)=\frac{e^{\beta_0+\beta_1X}}{1+e^{\beta_0+\beta_1X}}\). For the Default data ISLP reports \(\hat\beta_0=-10.6513\) and \(\hat\beta_1=0.0055\) (\(X\) = credit-card balance in dollars). What is the estimated probability of default at a balance of \(X=1000\)?

  2. The inside first. \(\hat\beta_0+\hat\beta_1X=-10.6513+0.0055\times1000=-10.6513+5.5=-5.1513\).

  5. One sentence. A person with a \$1,000 balance has an estimated 0.58% chance of defaulting; ISLP gets the same 0.00576 on p. 141. At \$2,000 the inside is \(0.3487\) and the probability is \(0.586\).

Exponentials and logarithms in one page

Two functions appear on almost every page of ISLP Chapter 4 and Prince Chapter 5, so they deserve a moment now (a full treatment, with derivatives, is in Derivatives and gradients).

  • \(\exp(x)=e^x\) with \(e\approx2.71828\). It is always positive, \(e^0=1\), and it grows faster and faster. Adding in the exponent multiplies the result: \(e^{a+b}=e^ae^b\).
  • \(\log(y)\) is the reverse: the power you must raise \(e\) to in order to get \(y\). So \(\log(e^x)=x\) and \(e^{\log y}=y\). It is defined only for \(y>0\), \(\log1=0\), and it turns products into sums: \(\log(ab)=\log a+\log b\).

Same log, different base. In communications you used \(\log_{10}\) (decibels) and \(\log_2\) (bits). In ML books, \(\log\) without a base is always the natural log, \(\log_e\) (Prince says so explicitly on p. 441). The bases differ only by a constant factor: \(\log_{10}y=\log y/\log 10=\log y/2.3026\).

def rss(b0, b1):
    xs = [1, 2, 3]
    ys = [2, 3, 5]
    total = 0
    for x, y in zip(xs, ys):

    return total

  3. \(\sum_i x_iy_i=(\sum_i x_i)(\sum_i y_i)\)
  4. \(\sum_{i=1}^n 3=3\)
A constant multiplier comes out of a sum. B and C fail as soon as there are two or more terms (try \(x=(1,2)\)). D: adding 3 \(n\) times gives \(3n\).

\(\epsilon\sim N(0,\sigma^2)\) is read…

  1. epsilon is approximately zero
  2. epsilon is an element of the set N
  3. epsilon is drawn from a normal distribution with mean 0 and variance sigma squared
  4. epsilon is proportional to sigma squared
\(\sim\) means “is distributed as”. “Approximately” is \(\approx\), “element of” is \(\in\), “proportional to” is \(\propto\).

Read this aloud and say what it computes, in one plain sentence: \(\displaystyle\hat\sigma^2=\frac{1}{n-2}\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\hat y_i)^2\).

“Sigma-hat squared equals one over n-minus-two times the sum, for i from 1 to n, of y-i minus y-hat-i, squared.” It is an estimate of the variance of the error: add up the squared residuals and divide by \(n-2\) (slightly fewer than \(n\), because two parameters were estimated). You will meet it in ISLP 3.1 as the square of the RSE.
In ISLP, what do \(n\) and \(p\) count?
\(n\) = number of observations (rows); \(p\) = number of predictors (input columns).
\(x_{ij}\): which comes first, row or column?
Row first: \(x_{ij}\) is observation \(i\), variable \(j\).
How do you say \(\hat\beta_1\), and what does the hat mean?
“beta-one-hat”. The hat means an estimate computed from data (of the unknown true \(\beta_1\)).
\(\min_x f(x)\) versus \(\argmin_x f(x)\)?
min = the smallest value of \(f\); argmin = the input \(x\) where that smallest value occurs.
What do Prince's square brackets in \(\mathrm{f}[\bx,\bphi]\) mean?
Function arguments, exactly like round brackets in \(f(x)\). Inputs: data \(\bx\) and parameters \(\bphi\).
Prince: Roman letters versus Greek letters?
Roman (\(x,y,h\)) = variables; Greek (\(\phi,\beta,\omega\)) = parameters.
Which logarithm do ISLP and Prince mean by \(\log\)?
The natural log, base \(e\approx2.718\) (ln on a calculator).
\(\sum_{i=1}^n c\,a_i=\)?
\(c\sum_{i=1}^n a_i\): a constant multiplier comes out of a sum.
\(\sum_{i=1}^n c=\)?
\(nc\)
\(\log\prod_i p_i=\)?
\(\sum_i\log p_i\): the log turns a product into a sum.
\(\|\bx\|_2\) and \(\|\bx\|_1\)?
\(\|\bx\|_2=\sqrt{\sum_j x_j^2}\) (length); \(\|\bx\|_1=\sum_j|x_j|\).
What does \(\Pr(Y=k\mid X=x)\) read as?
“The probability that Y equals k, given that X equals x.” The bar means “given”.
\(\sim\), \(\approx\), \(\propto\), \(:=\)?
is distributed as; approximately equal; proportional to; is defined as.
ISLP's \(n\) and \(p\) are called what in Prince?
\(I\) (number of training examples) and \(D_i\) (input dimension).
