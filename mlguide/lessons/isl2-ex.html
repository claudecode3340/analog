Conceptual exercises

For each of parts (a) through (d), indicate whether we would generally expect the performance of a flexible statistical learning method to be better or worse than an inflexible method. Justify your answer.

(a) The sample size \(n\) is extremely large, and the number of predictors \(p\) is small.
(b) The number of predictors \(p\) is extremely large, and the number of observations \(n\) is small.
(c) The relationship between the predictors and response is highly non-linear.
(d) The variance of the error terms, i.e. \(\sigma^2 = \Var(\varepsilon)\), is extremely high.

Hint

For each case ask two questions. Will a flexible method's variance be a problem (is there enough data to pin it down, or a lot of noise to chase)? Will an inflexible method's bias be a problem (is the truth far from simple)? Then use test MSE = variance + bias² + Var(ε).

Final answer

(a) Flexible better. (b) Flexible worse. (c) Flexible better. (d) Flexible worse.

Full solution, step by step
  1. (a) Large \(n\), small \(p\): flexible is better. Variance comes from fitting the noise of a particular training set. With an enormous number of observations and few predictors, each region of predictor space contains lots of data, so even a flexible fit is pinned down: its variance is small. Its bias is low too. An inflexible method keeps its bias however much data it gets (a line cannot bend no matter how many points you give it). So the flexible method can take advantage of the data and should have lower test error.Think of the green curve in Figure 2.9: with thousands of points instead of 50, it would stop wiggling.
  2. (b) Huge \(p\), small \(n\): flexible is worse. With few observations spread over many dimensions, a flexible method has too much freedom: it can fit the training data perfectly while learning mostly noise, so its variance is very high and it overfits. An inflexible method (e.g. linear regression with few parameters, or the lasso) has more bias but far less variance, and with so little data that trade usually wins.This is the high-dimensional setting of ISLP §6.4.
  3. (c) Highly non-linear truth: flexible is better. An inflexible method such as a straight line has large bias here, which no amount of data removes (Figure 2.11: the linear fit has test MSE near 19 against a floor of 1). A flexible method can follow the curvature, cutting bias dramatically, as in the right panel of Figure 2.12.
  4. (d) Very high \(\Var(\varepsilon)\): flexible is worse. When the noise is large, a flexible method chases it: each training set gives a very different fit, so variance is high. An inflexible method averages the noise away. Since the irreducible error is already large, the extra variance from flexibility costs more than the small reduction in bias gains.Signal processing view: in a low-SNR channel you use a long averaging (low-bandwidth) filter, not a wide-band one that lets the noise through.
Common mistakes
  • Answering (a) “flexible is worse because it overfits”: overfitting needs too little data for the flexibility; with very large \(n\) the variance shrinks.
  • Saying in (d) that the irreducible error itself grows with flexibility. \(\Var(\varepsilon)\) is the same for every method; what grows is the variance of \(\hat f\), because the flexible fit follows that noise.

We now revisit the bias-variance decomposition.

(a) Provide a sketch of typical (squared) bias, variance, training error, test error, and Bayes (or irreducible) error curves, on a single plot, as we go from less flexible statistical learning methods towards more flexible approaches. The x-axis should represent the amount of flexibility in the method, and the y-axis should represent the values for each curve. There should be five curves. Make sure to label each one.
(b) Explain why each of the five curves has the shape displayed in part (a).

Hint

Two curves go down, one goes up, one is flat, and one is U-shaped. The U-shaped one is the sum of three of the others. Figure 2.12 shows four of the five.

Final answer

Squared bias: decreasing. Variance: increasing. Training error: decreasing steadily (towards 0 for very flexible methods, below the Bayes line). Test error: U-shaped, always above the Bayes line. Bayes (irreducible) error: a flat horizontal line. Test error = bias² + variance + irreducible error.

Full solution, step by step

Rather than draw the curves by hand, we simulate them, so every curve is real data: polynomial fits of increasing degree to data from a curvy \(f\) with \(\Var(\varepsilon) = 1\), 300 training sets of 50 points.

f = lambda x: 4 + 5.5 * np.sin(np.pi * x / 130) ** 2
def fit_poly(x, y, d):
    V = np.polynomial.legendre.legvander((x - 50) / 50, d)
    c, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(V, y, rcond=None)
    return lambda z: np.polynomial.legendre.legvander((z - 50) / 50, d) @ c
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
x0 = np.linspace(5, 95, 37); degrees = range(1, 11)
res = {k: [] for k in ['bias2', 'var', 'train', 'test']}
for d in degrees:
    preds, tr = [], []
    for _ in range(300):
        x = rng.uniform(0, 100, 50); y = f(x) + rng.normal(0, 1, 50)
        fh = fit_poly(x, y, d); preds.append(fh(x0)); tr.append(np.mean((y - fh(x)) ** 2))
    P = np.array(preds)
    res['bias2'].append(np.mean((P.mean(0) - f(x0)) ** 2)); res['var'].append(np.mean(P.var(0)))
    res['train'].append(np.mean(tr)); res['test'].append(res['bias2'][-1] + res['var'][-1] + 1)
pd.DataFrame(res, index=pd.Index(degrees, name='degree')).round(3)
Each row is one flexibility level. Read down the columns: bias2 falls, var rises, train falls (below 1 once the fit can bend), test falls then rises, and the Bayes error is 1 throughout.
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 4.2))
d = list(degrees)
ax.plot(d, res['bias2'], 'o-', label='squared bias')
ax.plot(d, res['var'], 'o-', label='variance')
ax.plot(d, res['train'], 'o-', label='training error')
ax.plot(d, res['test'], 'o-', label='test error')
ax.axhline(1, ls='--', color='k', label='Bayes (irreducible) error')
ax.set_ylim(0, 2.8); ax.set_xlabel('flexibility (polynomial degree)'); ax.set_ylabel('error'); ax.legend();
This is the sketch the exercise asks for, made from data. In an exam you draw the same five shapes and label them.

(b) Why each curve has its shape.

  1. Squared bias decreases. Bias is the error from forcing a simple shape on a complicated truth. More flexible methods can take more shapes, so their average fit gets closer to \(f\). It flattens out near 0 once the method is flexible enough to represent \(f\).
  2. Variance increases. A flexible method follows the individual training points, noise included, so a different training set gives a noticeably different \(\hat f\). An inflexible method barely moves. Variance grows slowly at first, then fast.
  3. Training error decreases monotonically. A more flexible method can always fit the training data at least as well (it contains the simpler fits as special cases). Eventually it interpolates the data and the training error approaches 0, falling below the irreducible error: a sign that it is fitting noise.
  4. Test error is U-shaped. It equals bias² + variance + Var(ε) (2.7). At first bias falls faster than variance rises, so the test error drops; later bias is already small and variance takes over, so it rises. The bottom of the U is the best flexibility.
  5. Bayes (irreducible) error is flat. \(\Var(\varepsilon)\) is a property of the data, not of the method, so it does not depend on flexibility. The test error can never fall below it; the training error can, because it is measured on the data the method was fitted to.
Common mistakes
  • Drawing the test error dipping below the Bayes error line. It cannot: the other two terms of (2.7) are never negative.
  • Drawing the training error U-shaped like the test error.
  • Forgetting that the training error can go below the irreducible error.

What are the advantages and disadvantages of a very flexible (versus a less flexible) approach for regression or classification? Under what circumstances might a more flexible approach be preferred to a less flexible approach? When might a less flexible approach be preferred?

Hint

Use the three ideas of the chapter: bias, variance and interpretability. Then connect to the four cases of Conceptual 1.

Final answer

Flexible: + low bias, can fit complicated \(f\), often better predictions with lots of data. − high variance (overfitting), needs more data, harder to interpret, more computation. Prefer flexible when \(f\) is very non-linear, \(n\) is large relative to \(p\), noise is low, and the goal is prediction. Prefer less flexible when the goal is inference, \(n\) is small or \(p\) large, noise is high, or the truth is close to simple.

Full solution, step by step
  1. Advantages of a very flexible approach. It can represent a much wider range of shapes for \(f\), so its bias is low. If the true relationship is complicated (Figure 2.11), it can predict far more accurately than a linear method.
  2. Disadvantages. (i) High variance: it follows the noise, so it may overfit, giving low training error but high test error. (ii) It needs more data to estimate many parameters reliably. (iii) It is harder to interpret (Figure 2.7): you cannot easily say how each predictor affects the response. (iv) It is usually slower to fit and needs tuning (how flexible?).
  3. When flexible is preferred: the goal is prediction, not explanation; the true \(f\) is clearly non-linear; there are many observations compared with predictors; the noise level is modest. Example: image recognition with millions of labelled images.
  4. When less flexible is preferred: the goal is inference (we need interpretable coefficients); data are scarce or high-dimensional; the noise is high; or there is reason to believe \(f\) is roughly linear (Figure 2.10). Example: a medical study with 80 patients and 20 measurements.
Common mistakes
  • Claiming flexible methods always predict better. Figures 2.10 and 2.17 show otherwise.
  • Forgetting interpretability, which is often the deciding factor in practice.

Describe the differences between a parametric and a non-parametric statistical learning approach. What are the advantages of a parametric approach to regression or classification (as opposed to a non-parametric approach)? What are its disadvantages?

Hint

A parametric method makes an assumption first. What is it, and what does it buy you? Recall the two steps on p. 20 of ISLP.

Final answer

Parametric: assume a functional form for \(f\) with a fixed, small number of parameters, then estimate them. Non-parametric: no fixed form; let the data determine the shape, subject to smoothness. Parametric advantages: estimating \(f\) reduces to estimating a few numbers, so it needs fewer observations, is fast and interpretable. Disadvantage: if the assumed form is far from the true \(f\), the estimate is poor (high bias), whatever the sample size.

Full solution, step by step
  1. Parametric: two steps. (1) Assume a form for \(f\), e.g. linear: \(f(X) = \beta_0 + \beta_1X_1 + \cdots + \beta_pX_p\). (2) Use the training data to estimate the \(p + 1\) parameters, e.g. by least squares. The problem of estimating a whole function is reduced to estimating a few numbers.
  2. Non-parametric: no explicit form is assumed. The method looks for an \(\hat f\) as close to the data as possible while staying smooth (thin-plate splines, KNN). The number of effective parameters grows with the data.
  3. Advantages of parametric: (i) needs far fewer observations; (ii) estimates are stable (low variance); (iii) the parameters are interpretable (\(\beta_j\) is the effect of \(X_j\)), ideal for inference; (iv) fast to fit and to use.
  4. Disadvantages of parametric: the assumed form usually does not match the true \(f\) exactly; if it is far off, the fit is poor (high bias) and more data will not fix it. Trying to fix this with more flexible parametric forms adds parameters and risks overfitting.
  5. Example from the chapter: on the Income data, the linear plane (parametric, 3 parameters) missed the S-shape; the smooth thin-plate spline (non-parametric) captured it, but needed a choice of smoothness, and the rough spline overfitted.
Common mistakes
  • Saying non-parametric methods have no parameters at all. They usually have tuning parameters (like \(K\) or the smoothness); what they lack is a fixed functional form.
  • Saying parametric methods are always less accurate. When the assumed form is close to the truth, they are often the most accurate choice.

Hint

Euclidean distance in three dimensions: \(d = \sqrt{(x_1-0)^2 + (x_2-0)^2 + (x_3-0)^2}\). Because the test point is the origin, each distance is just \(\sqrt{x_1^2 + x_2^2 + x_3^2}\). The box above checks your distance for observation 3.

Final answer

(a) \(3,\ 2,\ \sqrt{10}\approx3.162,\ \sqrt5\approx2.236,\ \sqrt2\approx1.414,\ \sqrt3\approx1.732\). (b) Green (nearest is observation 5). (c) Red (neighbours 5 Green, 6 Red, 2 Red: Red 2/3). (d) Small \(K\).

Full solution, step by step

Write the six training points as the rows of a matrix and the test point as \(x_0\):

\[X = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 3 & 0\\ 2 & 0 & 0\\ 0 & 1 & 3\\ 0 & 1 & 2\\ -1 & 0 & 1\\ 1 & 1 & 1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad x_0 = \begin{pmatrix}0 & 0 & 0\end{pmatrix}\]

Common mistakes
  • Writing \(-1\) for the squared first coordinate of observation 5; it is \((-1)^2 = 1\).
  • Forgetting the square root. For ranking it does not matter, but part (a) asks for the distances.
  • In (c), choosing Green because the nearest neighbour is green. With \(K = 3\) all three vote equally.
  • In (d), answering “large \(K\)” because large \(K\) is “more powerful”. Large \(K\) is less flexible.
