Where are we? In Chapter 2 you met the least squares loss for a straight line. Throughout, the training data are \(I\) pairs \(\{\bx_i, y_i\}\) (read x-i, y-i): the \(i\)-th input and the output we want for it. A loss function \(L[\bphi]\) (read L of phi) takes the parameters and returns a single number. Small is good. Training means: find the \(\bphi\) that makes \(L\) as small as possible.

5.1 A change of view: the network predicts a distribution

Until now you have imagined the network spitting out an answer, "the house costs 31 lakh". Prince asks you to imagine something richer: the network spits out a probability distribution over possible answers, "the house most likely costs about 31 lakh, give or take 3". The single answer is still there (the peak of the distribution), but now we also have a shape that says how sure we are.

Why is this useful? Because once the output is a probability distribution, there is one obvious way to score it: how much probability did the model give to what really happened? If the true price was 30 lakh and the model's distribution puts plenty of probability near 30, it did well. If it put almost none there, it did badly. That single idea produces every loss in this chapter.

5.1.1 How can a network output a distribution?

The trick is pleasingly simple, and it is worth saying slowly because everything rests on it:

  1. Choose a parametric distribution \(\Pr(y\mid\btheta)\) that lives on the right set of values. For a real number \(y\in\R\) (read y in the real numbers) you might choose the normal distribution, whose parameters are a mean \(\mu\) (read mu) and a variance \(\sigma^2\) (read sigma squared).
  2. Let the network compute one or more of those parameters from the input. For example \(\mu=\mathrm{f}[\bx,\bphi]\), while \(\sigma^2\) is just some unknown constant.

So the network does not change at all. It still outputs ordinary numbers. We simply interpret those numbers as the parameters of a distribution. The network's weights are \(\bphi\); the distribution's parameters are \(\btheta\); the network turns \(\bx\) into \(\btheta\).

You already think this way in communications. A receiver does not see the transmitted symbol; it sees symbol + noise. The likelihood \(\Pr(\text{received}\mid\text{sent})\) is a Gaussian centred on the clean signal, and the maximum-likelihood detector picks whichever symbol makes the received samples most probable. For Gaussian noise that is the symbol at the smallest Euclidean distance. This chapter is that same detector, except the unknown is the network's parameters \(\bphi\) instead of the transmitted symbol.

5.1.2 The maximum likelihood criterion

For each training input \(\bx_i\) the model now predicts its own distribution parameters \(\btheta_i=\mathrm{f}[\bx_i,\bphi]\). Each observed output \(y_i\) should be probable under its own distribution \(\Pr(y_i\mid\btheta_i)\). We want this to hold for all \(I\) examples at once, so we choose \(\bphi\) to make the product of all those probabilities as large as possible:

Maximum likelihood criterion (equation 5.1)
\[\hat\bphi=\argmax_{\bphi}\Big[\prod_{i=1}^{I}\Pr(y_i\mid\bx_i)\Big]=\argmax_{\bphi}\Big[\prod_{i=1}^{I}\Pr(y_i\mid\btheta_i)\Big]=\argmax_{\bphi}\Big[\prod_{i=1}^{I}\Pr\big(y_i\mid\mathrm{f}[\bx_i,\bphi]\big)\Big]\]

Read: phi-hat (our chosen parameters) is the phi that makes the product, over every training example, of "the probability the model gives to the true output" as big as possible.

The product is called the likelihood of the parameters. The hat on \(\hat\bphi\) always means "the value we estimated", exactly as in ISLP's \(\hat\beta_1\).

Probability or likelihood?

Prince's footnote says a conditional probability \(\Pr(z\mid\psi)\) can be read two ways. As a function of \(z\) with \(\psi\) fixed, it is a probability distribution and adds up (or integrates) to one. As a function of \(\psi\) with \(z\) fixed (our data are fixed, the parameters vary) it is called a likelihood, and it does not generally sum to one. Same formula, different question.

Two hidden assumptions: i.i.d.

Writing the total probability as a product quietly assumes two things:

  1. Identically distributed: the form of the distribution is the same for every data point (always normal, say; only its parameters change with \(\bx\)).
  2. Independent: given the inputs, the outputs do not influence each other, so the joint probability splits into a product:
Independence (equation 5.2)
\[\Pr(y_1,y_2,\dots,y_I\mid\bx_1,\bx_2,\dots,\bx_I)=\prod_{i=1}^{I}\Pr(y_i\mid\bx_i)\]

Read: the probability of seeing all the outputs together equals the product of the probabilities of seeing each one on its own. This is the rule \(P(A\text{ and }B)=P(A)P(B)\) for independent events from the probability lesson, applied \(I\) times.

Together these are called i.i.d. (independent and identically distributed). Keep them in mind: when they are badly false (time series, repeated photos of the same person) the loss is still usable but it no longer means exactly what it claims.

5.1.3 Maximising the log-likelihood instead

Read the output: np.full(n, v) makes an array of n copies of v. The product is 0.0; the sum of logs is about −916. Taking logs turns a product of tiny numbers into a sum of moderate negative numbers.

The fix is to maximise the logarithm of the likelihood. The log of a product is the sum of the logs (\(\log(ab)=\log a+\log b\)), so:

Log-likelihood criterion (equation 5.3)
\[\hat\bphi=\argmax_{\bphi}\Big[\prod_{i=1}^{I}\Pr(y_i\mid\mathrm{f}[\bx_i,\bphi])\Big]=\argmax_{\bphi}\Big[\log\prod_{i=1}^{I}\Pr(y_i\mid\mathrm{f}[\bx_i,\bphi])\Big]=\argmax_{\bphi}\Big[\sum_{i=1}^{I}\log\Pr(y_i\mid\mathrm{f}[\bx_i,\bphi])\Big]\]

Read: the best phi is also the phi that maximises the sum of the log-probabilities of the true outputs.

Why is the answer the same? Because \(\log\) is monotonically increasing: if \(z>z'\) then \(\log z>\log z'\). So whatever change of \(\bphi\) makes the likelihood go up also makes the log-likelihood go up, and the peak of one is the peak of the other. (Throughout this book, \(\log\) means the natural log, base \(e\).)

Check it on the worked example: log-likelihood A \(=\log 0.78209+\log0.78209+\log0.73654=-0.24579-0.24579-0.30579=-0.79737\); log-likelihood B \(=-0.24579-0.54579-3.10579=-3.89737\). A is still better (−0.797 is bigger than −3.897), and nothing underflows.

5.1.4 Minimising the negative log-likelihood

Optimisers are written to minimise things. Multiplying by −1 turns "maximise the log-likelihood" into "minimise its negative". That gives the loss we will use everywhere:

Negative log-likelihood (equation 5.4)
\[\hat\bphi=\argmin_{\bphi}\Big[-\sum_{i=1}^{I}\log\Pr\big(y_i\mid\mathrm{f}[\bx_i,\bphi]\big)\Big]=\argmin_{\bphi}\big[L[\bphi]\big]\]

Read: the loss L of phi is minus the sum of the log-probabilities the model assigns to the true outputs. Each term is "how surprised the model was by the truth".

For our two lines: \(L_A=0.797\), \(L_B=3.897\). Small is good. The negative log-likelihood (NLL) of one example, \(-\log\Pr(y_i\mid\dots)\), is zero only if the model gave the truth probability 1, and it grows without limit as that probability heads to 0.

5.1.5 Inference: from a distribution back to one answer

After training, someone gives you a new \(\bx\) and wants a single prediction. Return the most probable value, the peak of the predicted distribution:

Point estimate (equation 5.5)
\[\hat y=\argmax_{y}\Big[\Pr\big(y\mid\mathrm{f}[\bx,\hat\bphi]\big)\Big]\]

Read: y-hat is the value of y where the predicted distribution is highest.

Usually there is a formula for the peak in terms of \(\btheta\). For a normal distribution it is the mean \(\mu\), which the network computed directly.

5.2 The recipe for building a loss

Here is the whole chapter on one card. Learn it by heart; you will apply it to every new problem you meet.

Recipe (equation 5.6)
  1. Choose a probability distribution \(\Pr(y\mid\btheta)\) defined on the domain of the outputs \(y\), with parameters \(\btheta\).
  2. Make the model predict one or more of those parameters: \(\btheta=\mathrm{f}[\bx,\bphi]\), so \(\Pr(y\mid\btheta)=\Pr(y\mid\mathrm{f}[\bx,\bphi])\).
  3. Train by minimising the negative log-likelihood over the training pairs: \[\hat\bphi=\argmin_{\bphi}\big[L[\bphi]\big]=\argmin_{\bphi}\Big[-\sum_{i=1}^{I}\log\Pr\big(y_i\mid\mathrm{f}[\bx_i,\bphi]\big)\Big]\]
  4. For a new input \(\bx\), return the whole distribution \(\Pr(y\mid\mathrm{f}[\bx,\hat\bphi])\), or the value where it is largest.

Distribution → network predicts its parameters → minimise NLL → read off the peak.

Interview classic: "Why do we use mean squared error for regression and cross-entropy for classification?" The strong answer is this recipe: MSE is the NLL of a Gaussian with fixed variance; cross-entropy is the NLL of a Bernoulli or categorical distribution. Then derive one of them on the whiteboard, as you are about to.

5.3 Example 1: univariate regression gives least squares

Univariate means one output. The goal: predict a single real number \(y\in\R\) from \(\bx\).

Step 1 of the recipe: choose a distribution on \(\R\). The natural choice is the univariate normal (Gaussian) distribution:

Normal distribution (equation 5.7)
\[\Pr(y\mid\mu,\sigma^2)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\!\Big[-\frac{(y-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big]\]

Read: the density at y equals one over root two-pi sigma-squared, times e to the minus (y minus mu) squared over two sigma-squared.

Take it apart piece by piece. \((y-\mu)^2\) is the squared distance from the centre. Dividing by \(2\sigma^2\) measures that distance in units of the spread. \(\exp[-\,\cdot\,]\) turns "far" into "small": it is 1 at the centre and falls towards 0. The fraction in front makes the total area under the curve equal to one, so a narrow curve (small \(\sigma\)) must be tall.

Tiny example. \(\mu=0,\ \sigma^2=1\), \(y=1\): \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-1/2}=0.39894\times0.60653=0.24197\). At the centre \(y=0\): \(0.39894\). Note a density is not a probability; it can exceed 1 when \(\sigma\) is small (with \(\sigma=0.1\) the peak is 3.99).

Step 2: let the network predict the mean. Set \(\mu=\mathrm{f}[\bx,\bphi]\) and keep \(\sigma^2\) as a constant:

Equation 5.8
\[\Pr\big(y\mid\mathrm{f}[\bx,\bphi],\sigma^2\big)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\!\Big[-\frac{(y-\mathrm{f}[\bx,\bphi])^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big]\]

Step 3: the loss is the NLL:

Equation 5.9
\[L[\bphi]=-\sum_{i=1}^{I}\log\Big[\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\!\Big[-\frac{(y_i-\mathrm{f}[\bx_i,\bphi])^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big]\Big]\]

5.3.1 From NLL to least squares, every step

Now the satisfying part. Watch the Gaussian NLL turn into the least squares loss you have used since ISLP Chapter 3.

Derivation · least squares is the Gaussian NLL (equation 5.10)
Start: \(\hat\bphi=\argmin_{\bphi}\Big[-\sum_{i}\log\Big[\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\big[-\frac{(y_i-\mathrm{f}[\bx_i,\bphi])^2}{2\sigma^2}\big]\Big]\Big]\). Write \(r_i=y_i-\mathrm{f}[\bx_i,\bphi]\) (the residual) to keep it short.
  1. Use \(\log(ab)=\log a+\log b\) inside the sum: \[\log\Big[\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\,e^{-r_i^2/2\sigma^2}\Big]=\log\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}+\log e^{-r_i^2/2\sigma^2}.\]The log of a product of two factors is the sum of their logs.
  2. Use \(\log e^{u}=u\): the second piece is just \(-\dfrac{r_i^2}{2\sigma^2}\). So \[\hat\bphi=\argmin_{\bphi}\Big[-\sum_{i}\Big(\log\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}-\tfrac{r_i^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big)\Big].\]The natural log undoes the exponential: that is why Gaussians and logs go so well together.
  3. The first piece, \(\log\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\), does not contain \(\bphi\). Adding the same constant \(I\log\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\) to every candidate's score never changes which candidate is smallest, so drop it: \[\hat\bphi=\argmin_{\bphi}\Big[-\sum_{i}-\tfrac{r_i^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big]=\argmin_{\bphi}\Big[\sum_{i}\tfrac{r_i^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big].\]argmin only cares where the minimum is, not its height.
  4. \(\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\) is a positive constant multiplying every term. Scaling a function by a positive number does not move its minimum, so drop it too: \[\hat\bphi=\argmin_{\bphi}\Big[\sum_{i=1}^{I}\big(y_i-\mathrm{f}[\bx_i,\bphi]\big)^2\Big].\]If \(g\) is smallest at \(\phi^*\), so is \(5g\) or \(0.1g\).
\(L[\bphi]=\sum_{i=1}^{I}\big(y_i-\mathrm{f}[\bx_i,\bphi]\big)^2\) — the least squares loss (equation 5.11).
Least squares loss (equation 5.11)
\[L[\bphi]=\sum_{i=1}^{I}\big(y_i-\mathrm{f}[\bx_i,\bphi]\big)^2\]

Least squares = "the outputs are independent and normally distributed around the network's prediction, with the same spread everywhere".

Check it numerically with the three-point example. The exact relationship (before dropping anything) is \(\text{NLL}=I\cdot\log\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}+\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\text{SSE}\), where SSE is the sum of squared errors. With \(I=3\) and \(\sigma^2=0.25\): \(3\times0.22579=0.67737\) and \(\frac{1}{0.5}=2\). Line A: SSE \(=0.01+0.01+0.04=0.06\), so NLL \(=0.67737+2\times0.06=0.79737\) ✓. Line B: SSE \(=0.01+0.16+1.44=1.61\), NLL \(=0.67737+3.22=3.89737\) ✓. The NLL is just a shifted, stretched copy of the SSE, so both rank the lines the same way.

5.3.2 Inference for regression

The network predicts \(\mu=\mathrm{f}[\bx,\bphi]\), and a normal curve peaks at its mean (equation 5.12, \(\hat y=\argmax_y\Pr(y\mid\mathrm{f}[\bx,\hat\bphi],\sigma^2)\)). So \(\hat y=\mathrm{f}[\bx,\hat\bphi]\): the network output is the prediction, just as before. Nothing about how you use a regression network changes; only our understanding of why the loss is right.

5.3.3 Estimating the variance too

The least squares loss does not contain \(\sigma^2\). But nothing stops us learning it: minimise the full NLL (equation 5.9) over both \(\bphi\) and \(\sigma^2\):

Equation 5.13
\[\hat\bphi,\hat\sigma^2=\argmin_{\bphi,\sigma^2}\Big[-\sum_{i=1}^{I}\log\Big[\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\!\Big[-\frac{(y_i-\mathrm{f}[\bx_i,\bphi])^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big]\Big]\Big]\]

Read: find the network parameters and the variance together. \(\hat\mu\) is the best prediction; \(\hat\sigma^2\) tells you how uncertain it is.

Prince stops there. Let us finish the job, because the answer is beautiful and you will reuse it. Write \(s=\sigma^2\). For fixed \(\bphi\), the NLL is \(\frac{I}{2}\log(2\pi s)+\frac{\text{SSE}}{2s}\). Differentiate with respect to \(s\) (derivatives lesson: \(\frac{d}{ds}\log s=\frac1s\), \(\frac{d}{ds}\frac1s=-\frac1{s^2}\)):

\[\frac{\partial\,\text{NLL}}{\partial s}=\frac{I}{2s}-\frac{\text{SSE}}{2s^2}=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \hat\sigma^2=\frac{\text{SSE}}{I}=\frac1I\sum_{i=1}^{I}\big(y_i-\mathrm{f}[\bx_i,\hat\bphi]\big)^2 .\]

The best variance is simply the mean squared residual. For line A: \(\hat\sigma^2=0.06/3=0.02\). (ISLP divides by \(n-2\) instead of \(n\) to remove bias; maximum likelihood divides by \(n\).)

# fit the line by least squares, then find the best sigma^2 by brute force
A = np.column_stack([np.ones_like(x), x])          # design matrix [1, x]
phi_hat = np.linalg.lstsq(A, y, rcond=None)[0]

5.3.4 Heteroscedastic regression: uncertainty that depends on the input

Assuming the same \(\sigma^2\) everywhere is called homoscedastic ("same scatter"). Often it is wrong: predicting a car's price is harder for rare luxury cars than for common hatchbacks. When the uncertainty changes with the input we call it heteroscedastic ("different scatter").

The fix: give the network two outputs. \(\mathrm{f}_1[\bx,\bphi]\) predicts the mean, \(\mathrm{f}_2[\bx,\bphi]\) the variance. One complication: a variance must be positive, and a network output can be anything. So we pass it through a function that always returns something positive. Prince squares it:

Heteroscedastic model (equations 5.14–5.15)
\[\mu=\mathrm{f}_1[\bx,\bphi],\qquad \sigma^2=\mathrm{f}_2[\bx,\bphi]^2\] \[\hat\bphi=\argmin_{\bphi}\Big[-\sum_{i=1}^{I}\Big(\log\Big[\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\,\mathrm{f}_2[\bx_i,\bphi]^2}}\Big]-\frac{(y_i-\mathrm{f}_1[\bx_i,\bphi])^2}{2\,\mathrm{f}_2[\bx_i,\bphi]^2}\Big)\Big]\]

Read: each point's squared error is divided by its own predicted variance, and a \(\log\) penalty stops the network from claiming huge variance everywhere.

Notice the balance. Dividing by \(\mathrm{f}_2^2\) lets the network "shrug off" a big error by predicting a big variance at that input. The \(\log\sqrt{2\pi\mathrm{f}_2^2}\) term charges for that shrug. The best trade-off is to predict a variance that matches the actual size of the errors at each \(\bx\). (In practice people often use \(\sigma^2=\exp[\mathrm{f}_2]\) or the softplus; any positive map works.)

# data whose noise grows with x: sd = 0.05 + 0.3*x
xh = np.sort(rng.uniform(0, 2, 200))
yh = 1 + 0.5*xh + rng.normal(0, 0.05 + 0.3*xh)
mu = 1 + 0.5*xh                                    # both models get the true mean
homo_s2 = np.mean((yh - mu)**2)                    # one variance for everyone
hetero_s2 = (0.05 + 0.3*xh)**2                     # "network" output f2 squared
def nll(y, mu, s2): return np.sum(0.5*np.log(2*np.pi*s2) + (y-mu)**2/(2*s2))
print("homoscedastic NLL  :", round(nll(yh, mu, homo_s2), 2))
print("heteroscedastic NLL:", round(nll(yh, mu, hetero_s2), 2))
Read the output: with the same mean, letting the variance follow the data lowers the NLL substantially. The least squares loss would score these two models identically, because it ignores the variance. Here we gave the "network" the true spread to isolate the idea; a real model would learn \(\mathrm{f}_2\).

Pitfall. If a heteroscedastic model ever predicts \(\sigma^2\to0\) at a point it fits perfectly, the NLL heads to \(-\infty\) and training blows up. Real code adds a small floor, e.g. \(\sigma^2=\mathrm{f}_2^2+10^{-6}\).

5.4 Example 2: binary classification gives binary cross-entropy

Now the output is a label \(y\in\{0,1\}\): is the restaurant review positive (1) or negative (0)? Is there a tumour in this MRI (1) or not (0)?

Step 1: a distribution on \(\{0,1\}\). The Bernoulli distribution has a single parameter \(\lambda\in[0,1]\) (read lambda): the probability that \(y=1\).

Bernoulli (equations 5.16–5.17)
\[\Pr(y\mid\lambda)=\begin{cases}1-\lambda & y=0\\ \lambda & y=1\end{cases}\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad \Pr(y\mid\lambda)=(1-\lambda)^{1-y}\cdot\lambda^{y}\]

The compact form is a switch: when \(y=1\) the first factor has power 0 (equals 1) and you get \(\lambda\); when \(y=0\) the second factor has power 0 and you get \(1-\lambda\).

Check with \(\lambda=0.3\): \(y=1\) gives \(0.7^0\cdot0.3^1=0.3\); \(y=0\) gives \(0.7^1\cdot0.3^0=0.7\). They add to 1 ✓.

Step 2: the network predicts \(\lambda\), but \(\lambda\) must lie in \([0,1]\) and a network output can be any real number. So squash it with the logistic sigmoid:

Logistic sigmoid (equation 5.18)
\[\sig[z]=\frac{1}{1+\exp[-z]}\]

Read: sig of z is one over one plus e to the minus z. It maps any real number into the interval from 0 to 1.

Numbers: \(\sig[0]=\frac{1}{1+1}=0.5\). \(\sig[2]=\frac{1}{1+e^{-2}}=\frac{1}{1+0.13534}=0.88080\). \(\sig[-1]=\frac{1}{1+e^{1}}=\frac{1}{3.71828}=0.26894\). Very positive inputs give nearly 1, very negative ones nearly 0. You met this exact function in ISLP logistic regression.

So \(\lambda=\sig[\mathrm{f}[\bx,\bphi]]\), and the likelihood of one label is

Equation 5.19
\[\Pr(y\mid\bx)=\big(1-\sig[\mathrm{f}[\bx,\bphi]]\big)^{1-y}\cdot\sig[\mathrm{f}[\bx,\bphi]]^{y}\]

Step 3: take minus the log and sum. Using \(\log(a^pb^q)=p\log a+q\log b\):

Binary cross-entropy loss (equation 5.20)
\[L[\bphi]=\sum_{i=1}^{I}\Big[-(1-y_i)\log\big[1-\sig[\mathrm{f}[\bx_i,\bphi]]\big]-y_i\log\big[\sig[\mathrm{f}[\bx_i,\bphi]]\big]\Big]\]

Read: for each example, if the true label is 1, pay minus log of the predicted probability of 1; if it is 0, pay minus log of the predicted probability of 0.

This is the binary cross-entropy loss (section 5.7 explains the name). For a point estimate, predict \(\hat y=1\) if \(\lambda>0.5\) and 0 otherwise.

5.5 Example 3: multiclass classification gives softmax cross-entropy

Now \(y\in\{1,2,\dots,K\}\) with \(K>2\) classes: which of the 10 digits is in this image? Which of 50,000 words comes next?

Step 1. The categorical distribution has \(K\) parameters \(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_K\), one probability per class:

Categorical distribution (equation 5.21)
\[\Pr(y=k)=\lambda_k,\qquad \lambda_k\in[0,1],\qquad \sum_{k=1}^{K}\lambda_k=1\]

Read: the probability of class k is lambda-k. All of them lie between 0 and 1 and add up to one.

Step 2. A network with \(K\) outputs computes \(K\) arbitrary real numbers. We need \(K\) numbers that are positive and add to 1. The softmax function does exactly that:

Softmax (equation 5.22)
\[\softmax_k[\bz]=\frac{\exp[z_k]}{\sum_{k'=1}^{K}\exp[z_{k'}]}\]

Read: softmax-k of z is e to the z-k, divided by the sum of e to every z. The exponential makes each entry positive; dividing by the total makes them add to one.

The prime in \(k'\) (read k-prime) is just a different letter for the counter inside the sum, so it does not clash with the \(k\) we are computing.

The likelihood that input \(\bx\) has label \(k\) is \(\Pr(y=k\mid\bx)=\softmax_k[\mathrm{f}[\bx,\bphi]]\) (equation 5.23). Minus its log, summed over the data, is the loss:

Multiclass cross-entropy (equation 5.24)
\[L[\bphi]=-\sum_{i=1}^{I}\log\Big[\softmax_{y_i}\big[\mathrm{f}[\bx_i,\bphi]\big]\Big]=-\sum_{i=1}^{I}\Big(\mathrm{f}_{y_i}[\bx_i,\bphi]-\log\Big[\sum_{k'=1}^{K}\exp\big[\mathrm{f}_{k'}[\bx_i,\bphi]\big]\Big]\Big)\]

Read: for each example, take the network's output for the true class, subtract the log of the sum of exponentials of all its outputs, and negate. \(\mathrm{f}_{y_i}\) means "the output whose index is the true label".

To get from the first form to the second, write \(\log\frac{e^{a}}{S}=a-\log S\). This is the multiclass cross-entropy loss. The point estimate is the class with the largest probability, \(\hat y=\argmax_k\Pr(y=k\mid\mathrm{f}[\bx,\hat\bphi])\), which is also the class with the largest logit (softmax never changes the order).

Sigmoid is softmax with two classes. Softmax of \((z,0)\) gives \(\frac{e^z}{e^z+1}=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}=\sig[z]\). So binary cross-entropy is the \(K=2\) case of multiclass cross-entropy, with one logit fixed at 0.

Temperature, and why the logits' size matters

If you divide every logit by a number \(T\) (the temperature) before the softmax, \(T\lt1\) sharpens the distribution and \(T\gt1\) flattens it. With our logits: \(T=0.5\) gives \((0.879,\ 0.119,\ 0.002)\); \(T=2\) gives \((0.547,\ 0.331,\ 0.122)\). Language models use exactly this knob when they generate text.

Numerical stability: subtract the maximum

The shift-invariance you just saw is not only cute; it saves programs. \(\softmax[\bz]=\softmax[\bz-c]\) for any constant \(c\), because \(\frac{e^{z_k-c}}{\sum e^{z_{k'}-c}}=\frac{e^{-c}e^{z_k}}{e^{-c}\sum e^{z_{k'}}}\). Choosing \(c=\max_k z_k\) makes the largest exponent \(e^0=1\), so nothing overflows.

5.6 Multiple outputs

Often one network predicts several things at once: a molecule's melting and boiling point (multivariate regression), or a class for every pixel of an image (semantic segmentation). The usual move is to treat the predictions as independent given the input, so the probability of the whole output vector is a product:

Independent outputs (equations 5.25–5.26)
\[\Pr(\by\mid\mathrm{f}[\bx,\bphi])=\prod_{d}\Pr\big(y_d\mid\mathrm{f}_d[\bx,\bphi]\big)\] \[L[\bphi]=-\sum_{i=1}^{I}\log\Pr(\by_i\mid\mathrm{f}[\bx_i,\bphi])=-\sum_{i=1}^{I}\sum_{d}\log\Pr\big(y_{id}\mid\mathrm{f}_d[\bx_i,\bphi]\big)\]

Read: \(\mathrm{f}_d\) is the group of network outputs that describes output number d; \(y_{id}\) is the d-th output of the i-th example. The log turns the product into a double sum: one term per example per output.

Examples. Ten continuous outputs, each normal with the network predicting the means: the loss is the sum of squared errors over all examples and all ten outputs. One class per pixel of a \(256\times256\) image: the loss is the sum of \(65{,}536\) categorical cross-entropies per image. Wind direction and speed: a von Mises term for direction plus an exponential term for speed (Problem 5.10). Different distributions simply add.

5.7 Cross-entropy and the KL divergence

You have probably heard "cross-entropy loss" more often than "negative log-likelihood". They are the same thing, reached from a different starting point. Prince's argument has three ideas.

Idea 1: a distance between distributions. The Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence measures how different a distribution \(p\) is from a distribution \(q\):

KL divergence (equation 5.27)
\[D_{KL}\big[q\,\|\,p\big]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}q(z)\log q(z)\,dz-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}q(z)\log p(z)\,dz\]

Read: "the KL divergence from q to p". It is zero when the two are equal and positive otherwise. For discrete distributions the integrals become sums.

The second integral, with its minus sign, \(-\int q\log p\), is the cross-entropy of \(p\) relative to \(q\). The first, \(\int q\log q\), is minus the entropy of \(q\). So KL = cross-entropy − entropy.

Worked example · KL divergence of two three-class distributions
\(q=(0.5,\ 0.25,\ 0.25)\) ("the truth"), \(p=(0.4,\ 0.4,\ 0.2)\) ("the model"). Discrete version: \(D_{KL}[q\|p]=\sum_k q_k\log\frac{q_k}{p_k}\).
  1. Ratios and logs: \(\log\frac{0.5}{0.4}=\log1.25=0.22314\); \(\log\frac{0.25}{0.4}=\log0.625=-0.47000\); \(\log\frac{0.25}{0.2}=\log1.25=0.22314\).
  2. Weight by \(q\): \(0.5\times0.22314+0.25\times(-0.47000)+0.25\times0.22314=0.11157-0.11750+0.05579=\mathbf{0.04986}\).
  3. Entropy of \(q\): \(-\sum q\log q=-(0.5\log0.5+0.25\log0.25+0.25\log0.25)=0.34657+0.34657+0.34657=1.03972\).
  4. Cross-entropy: \(-\sum q\log p=-(0.5\log0.4+0.25\log0.4+0.25\log0.2)=0.45815+0.22907+0.40236=1.08958\).
  5. Check: cross-entropy − entropy \(=1.08958-1.03972=0.04986\) ✓.
\(D_{KL}=0.04986\): small but positive; only \(p=q\) would give 0.

Idea 2: the data as a distribution. Put a spike of weight \(1/I\) at each observed \(y_i\). This empirical distribution is

Equation 5.28
\[q(y)=\frac1I\sum_{i=1}^{I}\delta[y-y_i]\]

\(\delta[\cdot]\) is the Dirac delta (read delta): an infinitely thin spike with area 1. Its key property: \(\int\delta[y-y_i]\,g(y)\,dy=g(y_i)\), it "picks out" the value of \(g\) at \(y_i\). (You know it from signals as the unit impulse.)

Idea 3: minimise the KL divergence from the data to the model.

Derivation · minimising KL = minimising NLL (equations 5.29–5.31)
Find \(\hat\btheta=\argmin_{\btheta}D_{KL}\big[q(y)\,\|\,\Pr(y\mid\btheta)\big]\).
  1. Write out the KL: \(\hat\btheta=\argmin_{\btheta}\Big[\int q(y)\log q(y)\,dy-\int q(y)\log\Pr(y\mid\btheta)\,dy\Big]\).
  2. The first integral involves only the data, not \(\btheta\). Drop it: \(\hat\btheta=\argmin_{\btheta}\Big[-\int q(y)\log\Pr(y\mid\btheta)\,dy\Big]\) (5.29). What is left is the cross-entropy.Same argument as before: a constant shift does not move the minimum.
  3. Substitute \(q\): \(-\int\Big(\frac1I\sum_i\delta[y-y_i]\Big)\log\Pr(y\mid\btheta)\,dy=-\frac1I\sum_i\int\delta[y-y_i]\log\Pr(y\mid\btheta)\,dy\).
  4. Use the picking-out property of the delta: each integral is \(\log\Pr(y_i\mid\btheta)\). So the objective is \(-\frac1I\sum_{i=1}^{I}\log\Pr(y_i\mid\btheta)\).
  5. Drop the positive constant \(\frac1I\): \(\hat\btheta=\argmin_{\btheta}\Big[-\sum_{i=1}^{I}\log\Pr(y_i\mid\btheta)\Big]\) (5.30).
  6. In machine learning \(\btheta\) is computed by the network: \(\hat\bphi=\argmin_{\bphi}\Big[-\sum_{i=1}^{I}\log\Pr(y_i\mid\mathrm{f}[\bx_i,\bphi])\Big]\) (5.31).
Exactly the negative log-likelihood of the recipe. Cross-entropy loss and NLL are the same loss.

That is why equations 5.20 and 5.24 are called binary and multiclass cross-entropy: for a single example whose true class is \(y_i\), the "true distribution" is a one-hot vector (all its mass on \(y_i\)), and its cross-entropy with the model's \(\boldsymbol\lambda\) is \(-\log\lambda_{y_i}\), our NLL term.

Read the output: scipy.stats.entropy(q, p) with two arguments computes the KL divergence, and agrees with the worked example. The second part fits the mean of a normal to ten points (positions like the arrows of figure 5.12a) by minimising the average NLL, which is the cross-entropy with the empirical distribution: the best \(\mu\) is the sample mean, −2.67.

Information theory, which you may have met in a communications course, gives the units: with natural logs, cross-entropy is measured in nats (with \(\log_2\), in bits). \(H(q)\) is the average code length of the best code for \(q\); cross-entropy is the average length if you design the code for \(p\) but the symbols really come from \(q\); KL is the wasted length. Training a classifier is building the best "code" for the labels.

5.8 Summary, and what the Notes add

We stopped thinking of a network as predicting \(y\) and started thinking of it as predicting the parameters \(\btheta\) of a distribution \(\Pr(y\mid\btheta)\). Maximising the likelihood of the training data is the same as minimising the negative log-likelihood, and that is the same as minimising the cross-entropy between the data and the model. Least squares falls out of a normal distribution with a predicted mean; letting the network also predict the variance gives uncertainty, even uncertainty that varies with the input. Bernoulli + sigmoid gives binary cross-entropy; categorical + softmax gives multiclass cross-entropy. Several outputs simply add their losses.

A few ideas from Prince's chapter notes are worth knowing by name: robust regression (a Laplace distribution gives the mean absolute error loss, which predicts the median and shrugs off outliers); quantile (pinball) loss for predicting, say, the 90th percentile; focal loss for heavily imbalanced classes; mixture density networks for multimodal outputs; and non-probabilistic losses such as the hinge loss of support vector machines. The next chapter finds the parameters that minimise these losses.

Three bugs that bite every beginner. (1) Applying softmax in your model and using a library loss that applies it again (PyTorch's CrossEntropyLoss expects raw logits). (2) Computing log(sigmoid(f)) literally, which gives -inf for large \(|f|\): use the logits version. (3) Comparing a summed loss with a mean loss and thinking training got 1000 times worse.

Product over \(d=1..10\), then logs, then the same two "drop the constant" moves as equation 5.10.

Final answer

\(\Pr(\by\mid\mathrm{f})=\prod_{d=1}^{10}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\big[-\frac{(y_d-\mathrm{f}_d[\bx,\bphi])^2}{2\sigma^2}\big]\); NLL \(=5I\log(2\pi\sigma^2)+\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_i\sum_d(y_{id}-\mathrm{f}_d[\bx_i,\bphi])^2\), minimised by the same \(\bphi\) as \(\sum_i\sum_d(y_{id}-\mathrm{f}_d)^2\).

Full solution, step by step
  1. Independence (equation 5.25): the likelihood is the product of 10 normal densities, one per output, as written in the final answer.
  2. Log of one example: \(\sum_{d=1}^{10}\big[-\tfrac12\log(2\pi\sigma^2)-\frac{(y_{d}-\mathrm{f}_d)^2}{2\sigma^2}\big]=-5\log(2\pi\sigma^2)-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_d(y_d-\mathrm{f}_d)^2\).
  3. Negate and sum over \(I\) examples: \(L=5I\log(2\pi\sigma^2)+\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^{I}\sum_{d=1}^{10}(y_{id}-\mathrm{f}_d[\bx_i,\bphi])^2\).
  4. With \(\sigma^2\) fixed, the first term is constant and \(\frac1{2\sigma^2}\gt0\) is a scale factor, so \(\hat\bphi=\argmin\sum_i\sum_d(y_{id}-\mathrm{f}_d)^2=\argmin\sum_i\|\by_i-\mathrm{f}[\bx_i,\bphi]\|^2\): a sum of squared terms (the squared Euclidean distance between prediction and target vectors).

Construct a loss function for multivariate predictions \(\by\in\R^{D_o}\) based on independent normal distributions with different variances \(\sigma_d^2\) for each dimension. Assume a heteroscedastic model, so both the means \(\mu_d\) and the variances \(\sigma_d^2\) vary as a function of the data.

Hint

The network needs \(2D_o\) outputs. Combine equation 5.15 with equation 5.26.

Final answer

With \(\mu_d=\mathrm{f}_d[\bx,\bphi]\), \(\sigma_d^2=\mathrm{f}_{D_o+d}[\bx,\bphi]^2\): \[L[\bphi]=\sum_{i=1}^{I}\sum_{d=1}^{D_o}\Big[\tfrac12\log\big(2\pi\,\mathrm{f}_{D_o+d}[\bx_i,\bphi]^2\big)+\frac{(y_{id}-\mathrm{f}_d[\bx_i,\bphi])^2}{2\,\mathrm{f}_{D_o+d}[\bx_i,\bphi]^2}\Big].\]

Full solution, step by step
  1. Distribution: \(\Pr(\by\mid\cdot)=\prod_{d=1}^{D_o}\Norm(y_d;\mu_d,\sigma_d^2)\) (a multivariate normal with diagonal covariance).
  2. Model: the network has \(2D_o\) outputs; the first \(D_o\) are the means, the next \(D_o\) are squared to give positive variances (or use \(\exp\), or add a small floor).
  3. NLL of one coordinate: \(-\log\Norm(y;\mu,\sigma^2)=\tfrac12\log(2\pi\sigma^2)+\frac{(y-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\).
  4. Sum over coordinates and examples (equation 5.26), substituting the network outputs: the final answer. Neither term can be dropped now, because the variances depend on \(\bphi\).
  5. Interpretation: each squared error is weighted by its own inverse variance, so dimensions (and inputs) the network declares "noisy" are trusted less, and the log term stops it declaring everything noisy.
Common mistakes

Dropping the \(\log\sigma_d^2\) term: then the loss is minimised by making all variances infinite.

A multivariate regression predicts a person's height in metres and weight in kilos from data \(\bx\). The units take quite different ranges. What problems do you see this causing? Propose two solutions.

Hint

Least squares assumes the same \(\sigma^2\) for both outputs. Is an error of 0.1 equally bad in both?

Final answer

The summed squared error is dominated by weight (errors of several kg) and almost ignores height (errors of a few cm = 0.0x m), because a shared \(\sigma^2\) is implicitly assumed. Fixes: (1) standardise each output (subtract its mean, divide by its standard deviation) before training, undo after; (2) learn a separate variance per output (Problem 5.8, even a homoscedastic per-dimension \(\sigma_d^2\)), which weights each squared error by \(1/\sigma_d^2\). Hand-chosen weights or a change of units are cruder versions of the same idea.

Full solution, step by step
  1. The problem in numbers. Typical spread: height sd ≈ 0.1 m, weight sd ≈ 15 kg. A mediocre model might be off by 0.08 m and 10 kg. Squared errors: \(0.0064\) vs \(100\). The weight term is about 15,000 times bigger, so gradient descent spends essentially all its effort on weight; height is learned poorly.
  2. Why, in recipe terms. Summing squared errors is the NLL of independent normals with the same variance for both outputs (Problem 5.7). That assumption is false here: weight is inherently far more variable (in its own units) than height.
  3. Solution 1: standardise the targets. \(\tilde y_d=(y_d-\bar y_d)/s_d\). Both outputs then have variance 1 and the equal-variance assumption becomes reasonable. Predict \(\tilde y\) and convert back: \(\hat y_d=\bar y_d+s_d\hat{\tilde y}_d\).
  4. Solution 2: learn the variances. Use \(L=\sum_i\sum_d\big[\tfrac12\log\sigma_d^2+\frac{(y_{id}-\mathrm{f}_d)^2}{2\sigma_d^2}\big]\) with learned \(\sigma_d^2\) (constants per output, or input-dependent as in Problem 5.8). The loss automatically divides each error by its own scale.
  5. (Also possible: measure height in centimetres. That changes the scale from 0.1 to 10, much closer to weight's 15: a quick fix that works only by luck of units.)
Common mistakes

Standardising the targets for training but forgetting to un-standardise predictions before reporting them.

With direction \(y_i\), speed \(s_i\gt0\), outputs \(\mu=\mathrm{f}_1\), \(\lambda=\exp[\mathrm{f}_2]\) (rate of an exponential, \(\Pr(s)=\lambda e^{-\lambda s}\)): \[L[\bphi]=\sum_{i=1}^{I}\Big[-\kappa\cos\big(y_i-\mathrm{f}_1[x_i,\bphi]\big)-\mathrm{f}_2[x_i,\bphi]+s_i\exp\big[\mathrm{f}_2[x_i,\bphi]\big]\Big]+\text{const}.\]

Full solution, step by step
  1. Distributions: direction \(y\in(-\pi,\pi]\): von Mises with mean \(\mu\), fixed \(\kappa\). Speed \(s\in\R^+\): exponential, \(\Pr(s\mid\lambda)=\lambda e^{-\lambda s}\), mean \(1/\lambda\).

In two or three sentences, explain to a friend why least squares is "the Gaussian loss".

If we assume each target is the network's prediction plus independent Gaussian noise with a fixed variance, then the probability of the data is a product of Gaussian densities. Taking minus the log turns each density into a constant plus the squared error divided by \(2\sigma^2\); constants and positive scale factors do not move the minimum, so maximising the likelihood is minimising the sum of squared errors.
