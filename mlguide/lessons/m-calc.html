Training a model means walking downhill on a loss surface, and derivatives are what tell you which way is downhill. This lesson rebuilds derivatives from the slope of a line, then adds partial derivatives, gradients, gradient descent and the chain rule, with every number shown, so that least squares in ISLP Chapter 3 and backpropagation in Prince Chapter 7 feel like old friends.

You'll use How to read the maths Vectors and matrices NumPy II matplotlib

By the end of this lesson you can

  • differentiate with the power, sum, product, quotient, chain, exp and log rules, including the sigmoid;
  • find a minimum by setting the derivative to zero, and prove that the mean minimises squared error and the median minimises absolute error;
  • compute partial derivatives and the gradient, and say why the negative gradient points downhill;
  • run gradient descent by hand for a few steps, and in NumPy;

  • derive \(\frac{\partial}{\partial\bbeta}\|\by-\bX\bbeta\|^2=-2\bX\T(\by-\bX\bbeta)\) and check any gradient with finite differences.
Most readers of this book will be accustomed to the idea that if we have a function y = f[x], we can compute the derivative ∂y/∂x, and this represents how y changes when we make a small change in x.

The derivative is a slope

For a straight line \(y=mx+c\), the slope \(m\) says how much \(y\) rises for each unit step in \(x\): rise over run. A curve has no single slope, but if you zoom in far enough on any smooth curve it looks straight, and that local slope is the derivative.

To measure it, pick two points on the curve, \(x=a\) and \(x=a+h\), and compute the slope of the straight line through them (a secant line). Then slide the second point towards the first by shrinking \(h\). The secant turns into the tangent, the line that just touches the curve at \(a\), and its slope is the derivative:

Definition of the derivative
\[f'(a)=\frac{df}{dx}\Big|_{x=a}=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}\]

Read: “f-prime of a”, or “d-f by d-x at a”, equals the limit, as h goes to zero, of f of a-plus-h minus f of a, all over h. The fraction is rise over run between two nearby points.

Worked example · the slope of \(f(x)=x^2\) at \(x=3\)
\(f(x)=x^2\), \(a=3\), so \(f(3)=9\).
  1. \(h=1\): \(\dfrac{f(4)-f(3)}{1}=\dfrac{16-9}{1}=7\).
  2. \(h=0.1\): \(\dfrac{f(3.1)-f(3)}{0.1}=\dfrac{9.61-9}{0.1}=6.1\).
  3. \(h=0.01\): \(\dfrac{9.0601-9}{0.01}=6.01\).The secant slopes are 7, 6.1, 6.01, … closing in on 6.
  4. Exactly, with algebra: \(\dfrac{(3+h)^2-9}{h}=\dfrac{9+6h+h^2-9}{h}=\dfrac{6h+h^2}{h}=6+h\). As \(h\to0\), this goes to \(6\).
  5. For any \(a\): \(\dfrac{(a+h)^2-a^2}{h}=2a+h\to2a\). So \(f'(x)=2x\), and at \(x=3\), \(f'(3)=6\).
\(\frac{d}{dx}x^2=2x\); at \(x=3\) the slope is 6: near \(x=3\), \(y\) rises about 6 times as fast as \(x\).
Move the point \(a\) and shrink \(h\). The orange line passes through the two points; its slope is printed next to the true derivative. As \(h\) gets small the orange line becomes the tangent (dashed). Try \(x^3-3x\) at \(a=1\): the tangent is flat, slope 0, because \(a=1\) is the bottom of a valley.

Three ways to write the same thing, all of which you will meet: \(f'(x)\) (Lagrange's prime), \(\frac{df}{dx}\) or \(\frac{d}{dx}f(x)\) (Leibniz's “d-f by d-x”), and in Prince \(\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\) even for one variable. A derivative is itself a function: \(f'(x)=2x\) gives the slope at every \(x\).

Read the derivative as a sensitivity. \(f'(a)=6\) means: “nudge \(x\) by a tiny amount \(\delta\), and \(f\) changes by about \(6\delta\)”. So \(f(3.001)\approx9+6\times0.001=9.006\) (true value 9.006001). This “small change in, proportional change out” reading is exactly how gradients are used in training.

You already know derivatives as rates: current \(i=C\,dv/dt\) through a capacitor, instantaneous frequency as the derivative of phase. And the finite difference \(\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}\) is the first-difference filter \(y[n]=x[n]-x[n-1]\) divided by the sample spacing: a crude differentiator, a high-pass filter. That is why numerical derivatives amplify noise.

The rules, each with a worked example

Nobody computes limits every time. A handful of rules, each proved once from the definition, lets you differentiate everything in months 1–3. Here they are, with \(c\) a constant and \(f,g\) functions of \(x\).

RuleFormulaExample
constant\(\frac{d}{dx}c=0\)\(\frac{d}{dx}7=0\)
power\(\frac{d}{dx}x^n=nx^{n-1}\)\(\frac{d}{dx}x^3=3x^2\); \(\frac{d}{dx}\sqrt x=\frac{d}{dx}x^{1/2}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt x}\)
constant multiple\((cf)'=cf'\)\(\frac{d}{dx}5x^3=15x^2\)
sum\((f+g)'=f'+g'\)\(\frac{d}{dx}(x^2+4x)=2x+4\)
product\((fg)'=f'g+fg'\)\(\frac{d}{dx}x^2e^x=2xe^x+x^2e^x\)
quotient\(\left(\frac fg\right)'=\frac{f'g-fg'}{g^2}\)\(\frac{d}{dx}\frac{x}{1+x^2}=\frac{(1+x^2)-x\cdot2x}{(1+x^2)^2}=\frac{1-x^2}{(1+x^2)^2}\)
chain\(\frac{d}{dx}f(g(x))=f'(g(x))\,g'(x)\)\(\frac{d}{dx}(3x+1)^4=4(3x+1)^3\cdot3\)
exponential\(\frac{d}{dx}e^x=e^x\)\(\frac{d}{dx}e^{2x}=2e^{2x}\) (chain rule)
logarithm\(\frac{d}{dx}\log x=\frac1x\)\(\frac{d}{dx}\log(1+x^2)=\frac{2x}{1+x^2}\) (chain rule)
Worked example · one example per rule, with a number at the end
Differentiate, then evaluate at the given point.
  1. Power and sum: \(f(x)=5x^3-2x+7\). Term by term: \(15x^2-2+0\). At \(x=2\): \(15\times4-2=58\).The sum rule lets you differentiate each term separately; the constant 7 does not change, so its slope is 0.
  2. Product: \(f(x)=x^2e^x\). With \(u=x^2\) (\(u'=2x\)) and \(v=e^x\) (\(v'=e^x\)): \(f'=2xe^x+x^2e^x=e^x(2x+x^2)\). At \(x=1\): \(e(2+1)=3e=8.155\).
  3. Quotient: \(f(x)=\frac{x}{1+x^2}\). Top \(u=x\), \(u'=1\); bottom \(v=1+x^2\), \(v'=2x\). \(f'=\frac{1\cdot(1+x^2)-x\cdot2x}{(1+x^2)^2}=\frac{1-x^2}{(1+x^2)^2}\). At \(x=0\): \(1\); at \(x=1\): \(0\).
  4. Chain: \(f(x)=(3x+1)^4\). Outside function “something to the 4th”, inside \(g=3x+1\). Derivative of the outside, evaluated at the inside, times derivative of the inside: \(4(3x+1)^3\times3=12(3x+1)^3\). At \(x=0\): \(12\).Check without the chain rule at \(x=0\) using a tiny step: \(\frac{(1.003)^4-1}{0.001}=12.05\approx12\).
  5. Exp: \(f(x)=e^{-x^2/2}\) (the shape of the bell curve). Chain rule with inside \(-x^2/2\), whose derivative is \(-x\): \(f'=-x\,e^{-x^2/2}\). At \(x=1\): \(-e^{-0.5}=-0.6065\).
  6. Log: \(f(x)=\log(1+x^2)\). Outside \(\log\), derivative \(1/(\text{inside})\); inside \(1+x^2\), derivative \(2x\): \(f'=\frac{2x}{1+x^2}\). At \(x=1\): \(1\).
\(58,\quad 3e\approx8.155,\quad 0,\quad 12,\quad -0.6065,\quad 1\)

The derivative of the sigmoid (you will use this constantly)

The sigmoid or logistic function \(\sigma(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}\) squashes any number into \((0,1)\). It is ISLP's logistic regression (ISLP 4.1) and an activation function in neural networks. Its derivative has a beautiful form.

Worked example · \(\sigma'(z)=\sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z))\)
\(\sigma(z)=(1+e^{-z})^{-1}\).
  1. Chain rule, outside \(u^{-1}\) (derivative \(-u^{-2}\)), inside \(u=1+e^{-z}\) (derivative \(-e^{-z}\)): \[\sigma'(z)=-(1+e^{-z})^{-2}\cdot(-e^{-z})=\frac{e^{-z}}{(1+e^{-z})^2}.\]
  2. Split the fraction: \(\dfrac{1}{1+e^{-z}}\cdot\dfrac{e^{-z}}{1+e^{-z}}\). The first factor is \(\sigma(z)\).
  3. Rewrite the second: \(\dfrac{e^{-z}}{1+e^{-z}}=\dfrac{(1+e^{-z})-1}{1+e^{-z}}=1-\dfrac{1}{1+e^{-z}}=1-\sigma(z)\).
  4. So \(\sigma'(z)=\sigma(z)\,(1-\sigma(z))\). At \(z=0\): \(\sigma=0.5\), slope \(0.5\times0.5=0.25\), the steepest point. At \(z=5\): \(\sigma=0.9933\), slope \(0.0066\): almost flat.Flat tails mean tiny gradients: the “vanishing gradient” problem that pushed deep learning towards ReLU (UDL 3, UDL 7).
\(\sigma'(z)=\sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z))\), at most 0.25

And the ReLU, \(\ReLU(z)=\max(0,z)\), Prince's favourite activation: slope 0 for \(z\lt0\), slope 1 for \(z\gt0\), and at exactly 0 it has a corner (no single slope; software picks 0).

Log and exp rules (worth memorising)

Rules for logarithms and exponentials
\[\log(ab)=\log a+\log b,\quad \log\frac ab=\log a-\log b,\quad \log a^k=k\log a,\quad e^{a+b}=e^ae^b,\quad \log e^a=a,\quad e^{\log a}=a\]

Logs turn products into sums, quotients into differences and powers into multipliers. Exps do the reverse.

Example: the log of a likelihood (Probability) \(\log\big(p^k(1-p)^{n-k}\big)=k\log p+(n-k)\log(1-p)\). A product of powers has become a sum, and sums are easy to differentiate term by term. With \(k=7,n=10,p=0.5\): \(7\log0.5+3\log0.5=10\times(-0.6931)=-6.931\), and indeed \(\log(0.5^{10})=\log(0.000977)=-6.931\).

def num_deriv(f, a, h=1e-5):
    return (f(a + h) - f(a - h)) / (2 * h)     # central difference (explained later)

Finding a minimum: set the derivative to zero

At the bottom of a smooth valley the tangent is flat, so the derivative is zero. That gives a recipe for minimising: differentiate, set equal to zero, solve. Then check it is a minimum and not a maximum or a flat shoulder: the slope should go from negative (downhill) to positive (uphill), which is the same as the second derivative \(f''(x)\) (the derivative of the derivative) being positive.

Worked example · minimise \(f(x)=(x-3)^2+1\)
\(f(x)=(x-3)^2+1\).
  1. \(f'(x)=2(x-3)\) (chain rule; the \(+1\) has slope 0).
  2. Set to zero: \(2(x-3)=0\Rightarrow x=3\).
  3. Check: \(f''(x)=2\gt0\), so the curve bends upwards: a minimum. \(f(3)=1\).
\(\argmin_x f=3\), \(\min_x f=1\)

The mean minimises squared error

Suppose you must summarise the numbers \(x_1,\dots,x_n\) by one number \(c\), and you are charged the squared distance \((x_i-c)^2\) for each. Which \(c\) is cheapest? This is the simplest least-squares problem there is, and its answer explains why the mean is everywhere in ISLP.

Worked example · \(\argmin_c\sum_i(x_i-c)^2=\bar x\)
\(L(c)=\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-c)^2\). Data for the numbers: \(x=(1,2,6)\).
  1. Differentiate each term with the chain rule (inside \(x_i-c\) has derivative \(-1\) with respect to \(c\)): \[\frac{dL}{dc}=\sum_{i=1}^n2(x_i-c)(-1)=-2\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-c).\]
  2. Set to zero: \(\sum_i(x_i-c)=0\Rightarrow\sum_ix_i-nc=0\Rightarrow c=\frac1n\sum_ix_i=\bar x\).\(\sum_i c=nc\), the sum rule from How to read the maths.
  3. Check: \(\frac{d^2L}{dc^2}=2n\gt0\): a minimum.
  4. Numbers: \(\bar x=(1+2+6)/3=3\). \(L(3)=4+1+9=14\). Neighbours: \(L(2)=1+0+16=17\), \(L(4)=9+4+4=17\). Both worse.
The mean \(\bar x\) is the least-squares summary: \(L(\bar x)=14\) for \((1,2,6)\).

The median minimises absolute error

Now charge \(|x_i-c|\) instead. The slope of \(|x_i-c|\) as a function of \(c\) is \(-1\) when \(c\lt x_i\) (moving \(c\) up brings it closer) and \(+1\) when \(c\gt x_i\). So

\[\frac{d}{dc}\sum_i|x_i-c|=(\text{number of }x_i\text{ below }c)-(\text{number of }x_i\text{ above }c).\]

This is zero (or changes sign) when as many points lie below \(c\) as above: at the median. For \((1,2,6)\), the median is 2: \(\sum|x_i-2|=1+0+4=5\), while at the mean, \(\sum|x_i-3|=2+1+3=6\). Note that the derivative does not exist at the data points themselves (each \(|x_i-c|\) has a corner there), which is why the median is found by sorting, not by setting a formula to zero.

Why this matters. Squared error punishes big misses heavily, so the mean is pulled towards outliers: change 6 to 60 and the mean jumps from 3 to 21, while the median stays at 2. The choice of loss is the choice of what “best” means. Prince Chapter 5 (UDL 5) shows that squared error corresponds to assuming normal noise, and absolute error to assuming heavier-tailed (Laplace) noise.

Drag the data points along the line. The top curve is \(\sum(x_i-c)^2\), the bottom one \(\sum|x_i-c|\); their lowest points are marked. The squared loss is a smooth bowl with its bottom at the mean; the absolute loss is made of straight pieces with its bottom at the median. Drag the right-hand point far to the right and watch which minimum follows it.

Partial derivatives: one input at a time

A loss depends on many parameters at once: \(L(\beta_0,\beta_1)\), or millions of weights in a network. A partial derivative asks a one-variable question of a many-variable function: how fast does \(f\) change if I move only \(x_1\) and hold every other input fixed?

Partial derivative
\[\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x_1+h,x_2)-f(x_1,x_2)}{h}\]

Read: “partial f by partial x-one”. The curly \(\partial\) (say partial or del) warns you that other variables exist and are being held still.

In practice: treat every other variable as a constant and use the ordinary rules.

Worked example · partial derivatives of \(f(x,y)=x^2+3xy+y^2\)
\(f(x,y)=x^2+3xy+y^2\); evaluate at \((x,y)=(1,2)\), where \(f=1+6+4=11\).
  1. With respect to \(x\) (treat \(y\) as a number like 5): \(x^2\to2x\); \(3xy\to3y\) (a constant times \(x\)); \(y^2\to0\) (a constant). So \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}=2x+3y\). At \((1,2)\): \(2+6=8\).
  2. With respect to \(y\) (treat \(x\) as a constant): \(x^2\to0\); \(3xy\to3x\); \(y^2\to2y\). So \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}=3x+2y\). At \((1,2)\): \(3+4=7\).
  3. Check with numbers: \(f(1.01,2)=1.0201+6.06+4=11.0801\), a rise of \(0.0801\) for a step of 0.01: slope \(\approx8.01\) ✓. \(f(1,2.01)=1+6.03+4.0401=11.0701\): slope \(\approx7.01\) ✓.
\(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(1,2)=8,\quad\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(1,2)=7\)

Picture a function of two inputs as a landscape: \(x\) and \(y\) are east and north, \(f\) is height. \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\) is the slope you feel walking due east; \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\), walking due north. A contour plot draws the landscape from above, with lines joining points of equal height, exactly like a hiking map.

x = np.linspace(-3, 3, 200)

The gradient: the uphill arrow

Stack all the partial derivatives into a vector and you get the gradient:

Gradient
\[\nabla f(\bx)=\begin{pmatrix}\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}\\[4pt]\vdots\\[2pt]\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_D}\end{pmatrix}\]

Read: “grad f” or “del f”, the vector of all partial derivatives. Prince writes it \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial\bx}\) (Appendix 23.5): a vector the same size as \(\bx\).

For our example, \(\nabla f(1,2)=(8,7)\). The gradient has three properties that make it the hero of machine learning:

  1. It points in the direction of steepest ascent. Of all directions you could step in, the gradient direction increases \(f\) fastest. So \(-\nabla f\) is the direction of steepest descent.
  2. Its length is the steepness: the rate of increase in that best direction, \(\|\nabla f\|=\sqrt{8^2+7^2}=10.63\).
  3. It is perpendicular to the contour line through the point (moving along a contour keeps the height the same, so it has zero slope).

Why the first property holds: for a small step \(\boldsymbol\delta\), \(f(\bx+\boldsymbol\delta)\approx f(\bx)+\nabla f\T\boldsymbol\delta\) (each partial derivative times its share of the step, added up). Among steps of fixed length, the dot product \(\nabla f\T\boldsymbol\delta=\|\nabla f\|\|\boldsymbol\delta\|\cos\theta\) is largest when \(\theta=0\), i.e. when \(\boldsymbol\delta\) points along \(\nabla f\) (Vectors and matrices), and most negative when it points the opposite way.

Worked example · using the gradient to predict a change
\(f(x,y)=x^2+3xy+y^2\) at \((1,2)\): \(f=11\), \(\nabla f=(8,7)\). Step \(\boldsymbol\delta=(0.01,-0.02)\).
  1. Predicted change: \(\nabla f\T\boldsymbol\delta=8(0.01)+7(-0.02)=0.08-0.14=-0.06\).
  2. Actual: \(f(1.01,1.98)=1.0201+3(1.01)(1.98)+3.9204=1.0201+5.9994+3.9204=10.9399\), a change of \(-0.0601\).The linear prediction is excellent for small steps; the tiny mismatch comes from the curvature.
Predicted \(-0.06\), actual \(-0.0601\)

Gradient descent

If you cannot solve \(\nabla f=\mathbf{0}\) with algebra (true for every neural network), you can still walk downhill: start somewhere, take a small step in the direction \(-\nabla f\), and repeat. This is gradient descent:

Gradient descent update (Prince eq. 6.3)
\[\bphi\leftarrow\bphi-\alpha\,\frac{\partial L}{\partial\bphi}\]

Read: “phi becomes phi minus alpha times the gradient of the loss”. \(\alpha\) (alpha) is the learning rate or step size, a small positive number you choose.

Worked example · gradient descent on \(f(x)=x^2\), by hand
\(f(x)=x^2\), \(f'(x)=2x\), start \(x_0=4\), learning rate \(\alpha=0.1\).
  1. \(x_1=4-0.1\times(2\times4)=4-0.8=3.2\).
  2. \(x_2=3.2-0.1\times6.4=3.2-0.64=2.56\).
  3. \(x_3=2.56-0.1\times5.12=2.048\).Each step multiplies \(x\) by \(1-2\alpha=0.8\), so \(x_t=4\times0.8^t\to0\), the minimum. With \(\alpha=1.1\) the factor is \(-1.2\): each step overshoots further, and the iterates explode.
\(4\to3.2\to2.56\to2.048\to\dots\to0\)

Now the real thing: fitting the line to our three points \((1,2),(2,3),(3,5)\) by gradient descent instead of the normal equations. The loss and its two partial derivatives (derived properly in the matrix section below) are

\[L(\beta_0,\beta_1)=\sum_i e_i^2,\quad e_i=y_i-\beta_0-\beta_1x_i,\qquad\frac{\partial L}{\partial\beta_0}=-2\sum_ie_i,\qquad\frac{\partial L}{\partial\beta_1}=-2\sum_ix_ie_i.\]

X = np.array([[1., 1.], [1., 2.], [1., 3.]])
y = np.array([2., 3., 5.])
beta = np.zeros(2)
alpha = 0.02
for t in range(2001):
    e = y - X @ beta                 # residuals
    grad = -2 * X.T @ e              # (dL/dbeta0, dL/dbeta1)
    if t in (0, 1, 2, 3, 10, 100, 2000):
        print(f"step {t:4d}  beta = {beta}  loss = {e @ e:.4f}")
    beta = beta - alpha * grad
Read the output: the first rows match the table. After 2000 steps \(\bbeta\approx(0.3333,1.5)\), the normal-equations answer, but notice how slowly the last digits arrive: the long narrow valley makes plain gradient descent slow. Prince Chapter 6 (UDL 6) is about faster variants (momentum, Adam).

The learning rate is the first thing to blame. Too small: progress crawls. Too large: the loss goes up and then becomes nan (“not a number”, the result of overflow). When a training run diverges, divide the learning rate by 10 before suspecting anything else.

The chain rule as a computational graph

Every model is a chain of simple steps: multiply, add, squash, compare with the truth, square. The chain rule says how a small change ripples through the chain: if \(y=f(u)\) and \(u=g(x)\), then

Chain rule
\[\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{dy}{du}\cdot\frac{du}{dx}\]

Read: “d-y by d-x equals d-y by d-u times d-u by d-x”. Sensitivities along a chain multiply: if \(u\) moves 3 times as fast as \(x\), and \(y\) moves 2 times as fast as \(u\), then \(y\) moves 6 times as fast as \(x\).

Draw the calculation as a computational graph: one box per elementary step. Then compute in two sweeps:

  1. Forward pass: left to right, compute every box's value from its inputs.
  2. Backward pass: right to left, compute the derivative of the final loss with respect to every box, by multiplying the incoming derivative by the box's own local derivative.

That two-sweep procedure is backpropagation, the algorithm that trains every neural network (Prince Chapter 7, UDL 7). Here it is for a single neuron with one input.

Worked example · backpropagation through one neuron, every number shown
Model \(a=\sigma(wx+b)\), loss \(L=(a-y)^2\). Data \(x=2\), target \(y=1\). Parameters \(w=0.5\), \(b=-0.5\). We want \(\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}\) and \(\frac{\partial L}{\partial b}\).
Graph: \(x,w,b\ \to\ z=wx+b\ \to\ a=\sigma(z)\ \to\ d=a-y\ \to\ L=d^2\).
  1. Forward: \(z=0.5\times2+(-0.5)=0.5\); \(a=\sigma(0.5)=\frac1{1+e^{-0.5}}=\frac1{1.6065}=0.6225\); \(d=0.6225-1=-0.3775\); \(L=(-0.3775)^2=0.1425\).
  2. Backward, start at the end: \(\frac{\partial L}{\partial L}=1\). Box \(L=d^2\) has local derivative \(2d\): \(\frac{\partial L}{\partial d}=2(-0.3775)=-0.7551\).
  3. Box \(d=a-y\): local derivative \(\frac{\partial d}{\partial a}=1\). So \(\frac{\partial L}{\partial a}=-0.7551\times1=-0.7551\).
  4. Box \(a=\sigma(z)\): local derivative \(\sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z))=0.6225\times0.3775=0.2350\). So \(\frac{\partial L}{\partial z}=-0.7551\times0.2350=-0.1774\).
  5. Box \(z=wx+b\): local derivatives \(\frac{\partial z}{\partial w}=x=2\) and \(\frac{\partial z}{\partial b}=1\). So \(\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}=-0.1774\times2=-0.3549\) and \(\frac{\partial L}{\partial b}=-0.1774\times1=-0.1774\).Both are negative: increasing \(w\) or \(b\) would raise \(a\) towards the target 1 and lower the loss. A gradient-descent step would do exactly that.
\(L=0.1425,\quad\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}=-0.3549,\quad\frac{\partial L}{\partial b}=-0.1774\)

A computational graph is a signal-flow graph. The forward pass is the signal; the backward pass runs the graph's transpose, and Mason's-rule-style products of branch gains along a path are exactly the chain rule. When paths split and rejoin, derivatives along the separate paths add (the multivariable chain rule), just as gains of parallel branches add.

Derivatives of vector expressions

Least squares, in matrix form, is \(L(\bbeta)=(\by-\bX\bbeta)\T(\by-\bX\bbeta)=\|\by-\bX\bbeta\|^2\). You will see its gradient everywhere (ISLP 3, ISLP 6.2 ridge, Project 1), so let us derive it twice: element by element with two parameters, then in matrix form.

Worked example · \(\frac{\partial}{\partial\bbeta}\|\by-\bX\bbeta\|^2=-2\bX\T(\by-\bX\bbeta)\)
Two parameters, \(\bbeta=(\beta_0,\beta_1)\), rows of \(\bX\) are \((1,x_i)\). Write \(e_i=y_i-\beta_0-\beta_1x_i\), so \(L=\sum_ie_i^2\).
  1. Partial with respect to \(\beta_0\): chain rule on each term, \(\frac{\partial e_i}{\partial\beta_0}=-1\): \[\frac{\partial L}{\partial\beta_0}=\sum_i2e_i\cdot(-1)=-2\sum_ie_i.\]
  2. Partial with respect to \(\beta_1\): \(\frac{\partial e_i}{\partial\beta_1}=-x_i\): \[\frac{\partial L}{\partial\beta_1}=\sum_i2e_i\cdot(-x_i)=-2\sum_ix_ie_i.\]
  3. Stack them and recognise a matrix–vector product. \(\bX\T\) has rows \((1,1,\dots,1)\) and \((x_1,x_2,\dots,x_n)\), so \[\nabla L=-2\begin{pmatrix}\sum_ie_i\\\sum_ix_ie_i\end{pmatrix}=-2\begin{pmatrix}1&1&\cdots&1\\x_1&x_2&\cdots&x_n\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}e_1\\\vdots\\e_n\end{pmatrix}=-2\bX\T\mathbf{e}=-2\bX\T(\by-\bX\bbeta).\]
  4. Set to zero: \(\bX\T(\by-\bX\bbeta)=\mathbf{0}\Rightarrow\bX\T\bX\bbeta=\bX\T\by\): the normal equations, now from calculus. The same steps work for any number of columns.
  5. Numbers, at \(\bbeta=(1,1)\) for our three points: \(\mathbf{e}=(0,0,1)\), so \(\nabla L=-2(0+0+1,\ 0+0+3)=(-2,-6)\).
\(\nabla_{\bbeta}L=-2\bX\T(\by-\bX\bbeta)\); zero exactly at the least-squares solution.

A few vector-derivative facts, each checkable element by element in the same way (with \(\mathbf{a}\) a constant vector and \(\mathbf{A}\) a constant matrix):

Function of \(\bx\)Gradient \(\frac{\partial}{\partial\bx}\)Scalar analogue
\(\mathbf{a}\T\bx\)\(\mathbf{a}\)\(ax\to a\)
\(\bx\T\bx=\|\bx\|^2\)\(2\bx\)\(x^2\to2x\)
\(\bx\T\mathbf{A}\bx\)\((\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{A}\T)\bx\) (\(=2\mathbf{A}\bx\) if \(\mathbf{A}\) symmetric)\(ax^2\to2ax\)
\(\|\by-\bX\bbeta\|^2\) (in \(\bbeta\))\(-2\bX\T(\by-\bX\bbeta)\)\((y-xb)^2\to-2x(y-xb)\)

When the output is a vector too, \(\by=\mathbf{A}\bx\), the derivative is a whole matrix of partial derivatives, the Jacobian. Prince's convention (eq. 23.21) puts \(\frac{\partial y_j}{\partial x_i}\) in row \(i\), column \(j\), which gives \(\frac{\partial\by}{\partial\bx}=\mathbf{A}\T\). Other books use the transposed layout; when in doubt, check shapes.

Numerical derivatives and gradient checking

You can always estimate a derivative with two function evaluations. The forward difference \(\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}\) has an error proportional to \(h\). The central difference \(\frac{f(a+h)-f(a-h)}{2h}\) is much better: its error is proportional to \(h^2\), because the curvature errors on the two sides cancel.

Central difference
\[f'(a)\approx\frac{f(a+h)-f(a-h)}{2h}\]

Read: “f of a-plus-h minus f of a-minus-h, over two h”: the slope of the secant through points just either side of \(a\).

But \(h\) cannot be made arbitrarily small: computers store about 16 significant digits, and subtracting two nearly equal numbers loses most of them. For \(f=e^x\) at 0 (true slope 1):

\(h\)forward errorcentral error
0.10.05170.00167
0.010.005020.0000167
\(10^{-5}\)\(5.0\times10^{-6}\)\(1.2\times10^{-11}\)
\(10^{-10}\)\(8.3\times10^{-8}\)\(8.3\times10^{-8}\)

Each tenfold cut in \(h\) cuts the forward error tenfold and the central error a hundredfold, until \(h=10^{-10}\), where round-off takes over and both get worse. \(h\approx10^{-5}\) with central differences is a good default.

Gradient checking uses this to test hand-written gradients: compute the gradient both ways and compare with a relative error \(\frac{\|g_{\text{analytic}}-g_{\text{numeric}}\|}{\|g_{\text{analytic}}\|+\|g_{\text{numeric}}\|}\). Below about \(10^{-7}\) the analytic gradient is right; above \(10^{-3}\) there is a bug. You will do this in the softmax classifier.

def loss(beta):
    e = y - X @ beta
    return e @ e

def grad_analytic(beta):
    return -2 * X.T @ (y - X @ beta)

def grad_numeric(f, beta, h=1e-5):
    g = np.zeros_like(beta)
    for j in range(len(beta)):
        step = np.zeros_like(beta); step[j] = h        # nudge only parameter j
        g[j] = (f(beta + step) - f(beta - step)) / (2 * h)
    return g

b_test = np.array([1.0, 1.0])
ga, gn = grad_analytic(b_test), grad_numeric(loss, b_test)
rel = np.linalg.norm(ga - gn) / (np.linalg.norm(ga) + np.linalg.norm(gn))
print("analytic", ga, " numeric", gn, " relative error", rel)
Read the output: both give \((-2,-6)\), as in the worked example, and the relative error is around \(10^{-11}\): the formula is right. grad_numeric works for any function of a parameter vector, which is what makes it such a useful bug detector.

Convexity: when “downhill” always finds the bottom

A function is convex if the straight line (chord) between any two points on its graph lies on or above the graph (Prince §23.1.2). In one variable, a twice-differentiable function is convex exactly when \(f''(x)\ge0\) everywhere: it always bends upwards, like a bowl.

  • Convex: \(x^2\), \(|x|\), \(e^x\), \(-\log x\), and every sum of convex functions. So the least-squares loss, the ridge loss and the lasso loss are convex.
  • Concave (upside-down bowl, chord below): \(\log x\), \(\sqrt x\).
  • Neither: \(x^3\), \(\sin x\), and the loss of almost any neural network.

Why it matters: a convex function has no separate local valleys. Any point where the gradient is zero is the global minimum, and gradient descent with a small enough learning rate gets there from anywhere. For neural networks this guarantee is lost, and Prince Chapter 6 (UDL 6) discusses why training works anyway.

Interview classics: “Derive the gradient of the squared loss for linear regression.” “What is \(\sigma'(z)\)?” “Why does gradient descent diverge with a large learning rate?” “How would you check that your backprop is correct?” (gradient checking with central differences). “Is the logistic regression loss convex?” (yes; a neural network's loss is not).

Ridge regression minimises \(L(\bbeta)=\|\by-\bX\bbeta\|^2+\lambda\|\bbeta\|^2\). Using the table of vector derivatives, find \(\nabla L\), set it to zero and solve for \(\bbeta\). For our three points with \(\lambda=1\) (penalising the intercept too, for simplicity), compute \(\hat\beta_1\) to 4 decimals.

Hint

\(\nabla L=-2\bX\T(\by-\bX\bbeta)+2\lambda\bbeta\). Setting it to zero gives \((\bX\T\bX+\lambda\mathbf{I})\bbeta=\bX\T\by\).

Final answer

\(\hat\bbeta=(\bX\T\bX+\lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1}\bX\T\by=(0.5,\ 1.3333)\).

Full solution
  1. Gradient (sum of two table entries): \(-2\bX\T(\by-\bX\bbeta)+2\lambda\bbeta\). Set to zero and divide by 2: \(-\bX\T\by+\bX\T\bX\bbeta+\lambda\bbeta=\mathbf{0}\), so \((\bX\T\bX+\lambda\mathbf{I})\bbeta=\bX\T\by\).
  2. With \(\lambda=1\): \(\bX\T\bX+\mathbf{I}=\begin{pmatrix}3+1&6\\6&14+1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}4&6\\6&15\end{pmatrix}\), and \(\bX\T\by=(10,23)\).
  3. Determinant \(4\times15-6\times6=24\). Inverse \(\frac1{24}\begin{pmatrix}15&-6\\-6&4\end{pmatrix}\).
  4. \(\hat\bbeta=\frac1{24}\begin{pmatrix}15\times10-6\times23\\-6\times10+4\times23\end{pmatrix}=\frac1{24}\begin{pmatrix}12\\32\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0.5\\1.3333\end{pmatrix}\).

The slope shrinks from 1.5 (least squares) to 1.3333: that is ridge regression's shrinkage (ISLP 6.2). Adding \(\lambda\mathbf{I}\) also makes the matrix invertible even when \(\bX\T\bX\) is not.

Why is the central difference \(\frac{f(a+h)-f(a-h)}{2h}\) more accurate than the forward difference, and why not use \(h=10^{-15}\)?

The central difference uses points symmetric about \(a\), so the curvature errors on the two sides cancel, leaving an error proportional to \(h^2\) instead of \(h\). But with a tiny \(h\), \(f(a+h)\) and \(f(a-h)\) agree in almost all of their 16 stored digits, so their difference is mostly round-off error, which is then divided by the tiny \(2h\) and blown up. \(h\approx10^{-5}\) balances the two errors.
