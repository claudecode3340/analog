Hint

Each row's p-value tests “this coefficient is zero given the other predictors are in the model”. Translate “\(\beta_{\text{TV}}=0\)” into words about sales.

Final answer

Four null hypotheses: (1) with zero spending on all three media, average sales are zero; (2) TV spending is not associated with sales once radio and newspaper are held fixed; (3) the same for radio, with TV and newspaper fixed; (4) the same for newspaper, with TV and radio fixed. Full solution, step by step
  1. Intercept. \(H_0:\beta_0=0\): “when the TV, radio and newspaper budgets are all zero, expected sales are zero.” p < 0.0001, so we reject it: even with no advertising, a market is expected to sell about 2,939 units.
  2. TV. \(H_0:\beta_{\text{TV}}=0\): “for markets with the same radio and newspaper budgets, TV spending makes no difference to average sales.” p < 0.0001: rejected. Holding the other two fixed, each extra $1,000 on TV is associated with about 46 more units sold.
  3. radio. \(H_0:\beta_{\text{radio}}=0\): “with TV and newspaper fixed, radio spending is not associated with sales.” p < 0.0001: rejected. About 189 more units per extra $1,000 on radio.
  4. newspaper. \(H_0:\beta_{\text{newspaper}}=0\): “with TV and radio fixed, newspaper spending is not associated with sales.” p = 0.86: we cannot reject it. The data give no evidence that newspaper advertising adds anything once TV and radio are accounted for.
  5. Conclusion. TV and radio are associated with sales; newspaper is not, after adjusting for the other two. (On its own, newspaper does look related to sales, because it is correlated with radio: the newspaper paradox.)
Common mistakes
  • Writing “newspaper has no effect on sales”. The test cannot prove zero; and it is conditional on TV and radio being in the model.
  • Forgetting the intercept's null hypothesis, or describing one null as “all coefficients are zero” (that is the F-test).
  • Saying the hypotheses in symbols only: the exercise asks for words about sales and budgets.

Carefully explain the differences between the KNN classifier and KNN regression methods.

Hint

Both find the same neighbours. What kind of response do they predict, and how do they combine the neighbours' responses?

Final answer

Both find the K training points nearest to \(x_0\). The classifier predicts a class: it estimates \(\Pr(Y=j\mid X=x_0)\) by the fraction of neighbours in class \(j\) and outputs the most common class (majority vote). Regression predicts a number: the average of the neighbours' responses, \(\hat f(x_0)=\frac1K\sum_{x_i\in\mathcal N_0}y_i\).

Full solution, step by step
  1. What is shared. Choose K. For a test point \(x_0\), find \(\mathcal N_0\), the K training observations closest to \(x_0\) (usually by Euclidean distance). Both are non-parametric: no formula for \(f\) is assumed.
  2. Response type. KNN classification is for a qualitative response (e.g. default yes/no); KNN regression for a quantitative response (e.g. sales).
  3. How neighbours are combined. Classifier: \(\Pr(Y=j\mid X=x_0)\approx\frac1K\sum_{i\in\mathcal N_0}I(y_i=j)\), then pick the class with the largest proportion. Regression: \(\hat f(x_0)=\frac1K\sum_{i\in\mathcal N_0}y_i\), the mean of their numbers.
  4. Output. The classifier's output is a label and its decision boundary is a set of regions; the regression output is a step-function surface (Figure 3.16).
  5. How they are judged. Classifier: error rate (fraction misclassified). Regression: mean squared error.
  6. Tiny example. Neighbours with responses (Yes, No, Yes) for classification → predict Yes (2 of 3). Neighbours with responses (3, 5, 4) for regression → predict 4.
  7. Same trade-off. In both, small K gives a flexible, low-bias, high-variance fit; large K a smoother, higher-bias one. Both suffer from the curse of dimensionality.
Common mistakes
  • Saying the classifier “averages the classes”: classes cannot be averaged; it takes a vote (proportions).
  • Thinking KNN regression fits a line through the neighbours: it only averages them.

Suppose we have a data set with five predictors, \(X_1=\) GPA, \(X_2=\) IQ, \(X_3=\) Level (1 for College and 0 for High School), \(X_4=\) Interaction between GPA and IQ, and \(X_5=\) Interaction between GPA and Level. The response is starting salary after graduation (in thousands of dollars). Suppose we use least squares to fit the model, and get \(\hat\beta_0=50\), \(\hat\beta_1=20\), \(\hat\beta_2=0.07\), \(\hat\beta_3=35\), \(\hat\beta_4=0.01\), \(\hat\beta_5=-10\).

(a) Which answer is correct, and why?

  1. For a fixed value of IQ and GPA, high school graduates earn more, on average, than college graduates.
  2. For a fixed value of IQ and GPA, college graduates earn more, on average, than high school graduates.
  3. For a fixed value of IQ and GPA, high school graduates earn more, on average, than college graduates provided that the GPA is high enough.
  4. For a fixed value of IQ and GPA, college graduates earn more, on average, than high school graduates provided that the GPA is high enough.

(b) Predict the salary of a college graduate with IQ of 110 and a GPA of 4.0.

(c) True or false: Since the coefficient for the GPA/IQ interaction term is very small, there is very little evidence of an interaction effect. Justify your answer.

Hint

Write the model out and subtract the high-school prediction from the college prediction at the same GPA and IQ: everything without Level cancels.

Final answer

(a) iii: college minus high school \(=35-10\times\text{GPA}\), negative once GPA > 3.5. (b) \(\hat y=137.1\), i.e. $137,100. (c) False: the size of a coefficient is not evidence; you need its standard error / p-value. And GPA × IQ is a large number (about 400), so 0.01 per unit is about 4 thousand dollars.

Full solution, step by step
  1. The model. \(\hat y=50+20\,\text{GPA}+0.07\,\text{IQ}+35\,\text{Level}+0.01\,(\text{GPA}\times\text{IQ})-10\,(\text{GPA}\times\text{Level})\).
  2. (a) High school (Level = 0): \(50+20\,\text{GPA}+0.07\,\text{IQ}+0.01\,\text{GPA}\cdot\text{IQ}\). College (Level = 1): the same plus \(35-10\,\text{GPA}\).
  3. Difference (college − high school) \(=35-10\,\text{GPA}\). It is positive when GPA < 3.5 and negative when GPA > 3.5. So at fixed IQ and GPA, high-school graduates earn more on average provided the GPA is high enough (above 3.5): option iii. Options i and ii are wrong because the sign depends on GPA; iv has the direction reversed.
  4. (b) GPA = 4.0, IQ = 110, Level = 1: \[\hat y=50+20(4)+0.07(110)+35(1)+0.01(4\times110)-10(4\times1)=50+80+7.7+35+4.4-40=137.1.\] Salary is in thousands: $137,100.
  5. (c) False. Whether there is evidence for an interaction is judged by the t-statistic \(\hat\beta_4/\SE(\hat\beta_4)\) and its p-value, which we are not given. A small coefficient with a tiny SE can be highly significant. Moreover the coefficient's size depends on units: GPA × IQ is around \(4\times110=440\), so the term contributes \(0.01\times440=4.4\) thousand dollars, as part (b) showed. That is not negligible.
Common mistakes
  • Choosing ii because \(\hat\beta_3=35\) is positive, forgetting the GPA × Level term.
  • In (b), forgetting that salary is in thousands, or using Level = 0.
  • In (c), judging significance by the size of the coefficient.

I collect a set of data (\(n=100\) observations) containing a single predictor and a quantitative response. I then fit a linear regression model to the data, as well as a separate cubic regression, i.e. \(Y=\beta_0+\beta_1X+\beta_2X^2+\beta_3X^3+\epsilon\).

(a) Suppose that the true relationship between \(X\) and \(Y\) is linear, i.e. \(Y=\beta_0+\beta_1X+\epsilon\). Consider the training residual sum of squares (RSS) for the linear regression, and also the training RSS for the cubic regression. Would we expect one to be lower than the other, would we expect them to be the same, or is there not enough information to tell? Justify your answer.

(b) Answer (a) using test rather than training RSS.

(c) Suppose that the true relationship between \(X\) and \(Y\) is not linear, but we don't know how far it is from linear. Consider the training RSS for the linear regression, and also the training RSS for the cubic regression. Would we expect one to be lower than the other, would we expect them to be the same, or is there not enough information to tell? Justify your answer.

(d) Answer (c) using test rather than training RSS.

Hint

The linear model is a special case of the cubic (set \(\beta_2=\beta_3=0\)). What does that imply for the training RSS? For test RSS, think bias–variance.

Final answer

(a) Cubic training RSS ≤ linear (almost always strictly lower). (b) Expect the linear model's test RSS to be lower: the cubic adds variance without reducing bias. (c) Cubic training RSS ≤ linear, always. (d) Not enough information: it depends on how non-linear the truth is.

Full solution, step by step
  1. Key fact. The two models are nested: any line \(\beta_0+\beta_1X\) is a cubic with \(\beta_2=\beta_3=0\). Least squares on the cubic searches a bigger set of curves that includes every line, so its minimum training RSS can only be lower or equal.
  2. (a) Even though the truth is linear, the cubic will use \(\beta_2,\beta_3\) to chase the noise in the 100 points, so its training RSS is lower (equal only in the freak case where the best cubic coefficients are exactly 0).
  3. (b) On new data, the linear model has no bias (it has the right form) and lower variance. The cubic has the same zero bias but extra variance from fitting noise, so we expect the linear model's test RSS to be lower. It is an expectation, not a guarantee: with a particular test set it could go either way.
  4. (c) Same nesting argument: the cubic's training RSS is lower or equal whatever the truth is.
  5. (d) Now the linear model has bias. If the truth is strongly non-linear, the cubic's reduction in bias outweighs its extra variance and its test RSS is lower. If the truth is only very slightly non-linear, the linear model's lower variance may win. Without knowing how far from linear the truth is, there is not enough information to tell.

The code below checks (a) and (b) by simulation.

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
wins_train, wins_test = 0, 0
for rep in range(500):
    x = rng.normal(size=100); y = 1 + 2 * x + rng.normal(size=100)          # linear truth
    xt = rng.normal(size=1000); yt = 1 + 2 * xt + rng.normal(size=1000)     # test data
    c1, c3 = np.polyfit(x, y, 1), np.polyfit(x, y, 3)
    tr1, tr3 = np.sum((y - np.polyval(c1, x))**2), np.sum((y - np.polyval(c3, x))**2)
    te1, te3 = np.sum((yt - np.polyval(c1, xt))**2), np.sum((yt - np.polyval(c3, xt))**2)
    wins_train += tr3 <= tr1; wins_test += te1 < te3
print("cubic training RSS <= linear in", wins_train, "of 500 data sets")
print("linear test RSS < cubic in", wins_test, "of 500 data sets")
Read the output: the cubic's training RSS is lower in all 500 data sets, and the linear model's test RSS is lower in most but not all of them, exactly as argued. np.polyval(c, x) evaluates the polynomial with coefficients c at x; adding a True/False to a number counts True as 1.
Common mistakes
  • In (a), answering “the same, because the truth is linear”: training RSS never rewards the right model, it rewards flexibility.
  • In (b), claiming the linear test RSS is always lower: it is lower on average.
  • In (d), confidently picking the cubic: a nearly linear truth can still favour the line.

Consider the fitted values that result from performing linear regression without an intercept. In this setting, the \(i\)th fitted value takes the form \(\hat y_i=x_i\hat\beta\), where \[\hat\beta=\left(\sum_{i=1}^n x_iy_i\right)\Big/\left(\sum_{i'=1}^n x_{i'}^2\right).\qquad(3.38)\] Show that we can write \(\hat y_i=\sum_{i'=1}^n a_{i'}y_{i'}\). What is \(a_{i'}\)?

Note: we interpret this result by saying that the fitted values from linear regression are linear combinations of the response values.

Hint

The index \(i\) inside the sum in \(\hat\beta\) is a dummy index: rename it \(i'\) so it does not clash with the \(i\) of \(\hat y_i\). Rename the one in the denominator \(k\).

Final answer
\[a_{i'}=\frac{x_i\,x_{i'}}{\sum_{k=1}^n x_k^2}\]
Full solution, step by step
  1. Rename dummy indices. A summation index is a placeholder; any letter works. Write \(\hat\beta=\dfrac{\sum_{i'=1}^n x_{i'}y_{i'}}{\sum_{k=1}^n x_k^2}\).This avoids confusing the fixed \(i\) (the observation whose fitted value we want) with the running index of the sum.
  2. Substitute. \(\hat y_i=x_i\hat\beta=x_i\,\dfrac{\sum_{i'}x_{i'}y_{i'}}{\sum_k x_k^2}\).
  3. Move the constants inside the sum. \(x_i\) and \(\sum_kx_k^2\) do not depend on \(i'\), so they can go inside: \[\hat y_i=\sum_{i'=1}^n\left(\frac{x_ix_{i'}}{\sum_kx_k^2}\right)y_{i'}.\]
  4. Read off. This has the form \(\sum_{i'}a_{i'}y_{i'}\) with \(a_{i'}=\dfrac{x_ix_{i'}}{\sum_kx_k^2}\). The weights depend only on the \(x\)'s, never on the \(y\)'s.
  5. Tiny check. \(x=(1,2)\), \(y=(3,5)\): \(\hat\beta=(3+10)/(1+4)=2.6\), so \(\hat y_2=2\times2.6=5.2\). Weights for \(i=2\): \(a_1=2\cdot1/5=0.4\), \(a_2=2\cdot2/5=0.8\); \(0.4\times3+0.8\times5=1.2+4=5.2\). ✓
  6. In matrix form (any linear regression): \(\hat\by=\bX(\bX\T\bX)^{-1}\bX\T\by=\mathbf{H}\by\). Row \(i\) of the hat matrix \(\mathbf{H}\) holds the weights \(a_{i'}\); its diagonal entries are the leverages \(h_i\). Here \(h_i=a_i=x_i^2/\sum_kx_k^2\).
Common mistakes
  • Keeping the same letter \(i\) inside and outside the sum, which leads to nonsense like \(a_i=x_i^2/\sum x_i^2\) for every weight.
  • Putting \(y\)'s inside \(a_{i'}\): the weights must not depend on the responses.

Using (3.4), argue that in the case of simple linear regression, the least squares line always passes through the point \((\bar x,\bar y)\).

Hint

Evaluate the fitted line at \(x=\bar x\) and use the formula for \(\hat\beta_0\).

Final answer

At \(x=\bar x\): \(\hat y=\hat\beta_0+\hat\beta_1\bar x=(\bar y-\hat\beta_1\bar x)+\hat\beta_1\bar x=\bar y\).

Full solution, step by step
  1. From (3.4): \(\hat\beta_0=\bar y-\hat\beta_1\bar x\).
  2. The least-squares line is \(\hat y(x)=\hat\beta_0+\hat\beta_1x\). Put \(x=\bar x\): \(\hat y(\bar x)=\hat\beta_0+\hat\beta_1\bar x\).
  3. Substitute step 1: \(\hat y(\bar x)=\bar y-\hat\beta_1\bar x+\hat\beta_1\bar x=\bar y\).
  4. So the point \((\bar x,\bar y)\) lies on the line, for any data set (as long as \(\hat\beta_1\) exists, i.e. the \(x\)'s are not all equal). Check with lesson 3.1's five points: \(2.2+0.6\times3=4=\bar y\). ✓
Common mistakes
  • Proving it only for one numerical example: the exercise asks for a general argument.

It is claimed in the text that in the case of simple linear regression of \(Y\) onto \(X\), the \(R^2\) statistic (3.17) is equal to the square of the correlation between \(X\) and \(Y\) (3.18). Prove that this is the case. For simplicity, you may assume that \(\bar x=\bar y=0\).

Hint

With \(\bar x=\bar y=0\): \(\hat\beta_0=0\), \(\hat\beta_1=\sum x_iy_i/\sum x_i^2\), \(\text{TSS}=\sum y_i^2\), \(r=\sum x_iy_i/\sqrt{\sum x_i^2\sum y_i^2}\). Expand RSS.

Final answer

\(\RSS=\sum y_i^2-\frac{(\sum x_iy_i)^2}{\sum x_i^2}\), so \(R^2=1-\frac{\RSS}{\text{TSS}}=\frac{(\sum x_iy_i)^2}{\sum x_i^2\sum y_i^2}=r^2\).

Full solution, step by step
  1. Simplify with zero means. \(\hat\beta_0=\bar y-\hat\beta_1\bar x=0\). \(\hat\beta_1=\dfrac{\sum(x_i-0)(y_i-0)}{\sum(x_i-0)^2}=\dfrac{S}{X}\), where we write \(S=\sum x_iy_i\), \(X=\sum x_i^2\), \(Y=\sum y_i^2\) to keep the algebra light. Fitted values: \(\hat y_i=\hat\beta_1x_i\).
  2. Total sum of squares. \(\text{TSS}=\sum(y_i-\bar y)^2=\sum y_i^2=Y\).
  3. Expand RSS. \[\RSS=\sum(y_i-\hat\beta_1x_i)^2=\sum y_i^2-2\hat\beta_1\sum x_iy_i+\hat\beta_1^2\sum x_i^2=Y-2\frac{S}{X}S+\frac{S^2}{X^2}X.\]
  4. Simplify. \(Y-\frac{2S^2}{X}+\frac{S^2}{X}=Y-\frac{S^2}{X}\).
  5. \(R^2\). \(R^2=1-\frac{\RSS}{\text{TSS}}=1-\frac{Y-S^2/X}{Y}=\frac{S^2/X}{Y}=\frac{S^2}{XY}\).
  6. Correlation. With zero means, (3.18) is \(r=\dfrac{\sum x_iy_i}{\sqrt{\sum x_i^2}\sqrt{\sum y_i^2}}=\dfrac{S}{\sqrt{XY}}\), so \(r^2=\dfrac{S^2}{XY}\).
  7. Compare steps 5 and 6: \(R^2=r^2\). ∎Why the zero-mean assumption costs nothing: subtracting the means from \(x\) and \(y\) changes neither \(R^2\) nor \(r\), so the proof covers every data set.
rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
x = rng.normal(size=50); y = 3 - 2 * x + rng.normal(size=50)
r = np.corrcoef(x, y)[0, 1]
R2 = sm.OLS(y, sm.add_constant(x)).fit().rsquared
print(f"r = {r:.5f}, r^2 = {r**2:.5f}, R^2 = {R2:.5f}")
Read the output: a numerical check on random data (with non-zero means): \(r^2\) and \(R^2\) agree to every digit, and \(r\) is negative because the slope is negative.
Common mistakes
  • Expanding \((y_i-\hat\beta_1x_i)^2\) and forgetting the factor 2 in the cross term.
  • Writing TSS as \(\sum(y_i-\hat y_i)^2\): that is RSS.
  • Concluding \(R^2=r\): the correlation must be squared (\(r\) can be negative, \(R^2\) cannot).
