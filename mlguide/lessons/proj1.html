Step 1 · The design matrix

The model from ISLP 3.2 is

Multiple linear regression for Advertising
\[\text{sales}_i \approx \beta_0 + \beta_1\,\text{TV}_i + \beta_2\,\text{radio}_i + \beta_3\,\text{newspaper}_i\]

Read: the sales of market \(i\) are roughly beta-nought, plus beta-one times its TV budget, plus beta-two times its radio budget, plus beta-three times its newspaper budget.

Writing that sum out 200 times is tedious. Linear algebra lets us write all 200 equations at once. We stack the inputs into a table of numbers called the design matrix \(\bX\) (read bold X), stack the outputs into a vector \(\by\) (bold y), and stack the coefficients into a vector \(\bbeta\) (bold beta):

All the equations at once
\[\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} y_1\\ y_2\\ \vdots\\ y_n\end{pmatrix}}_{\by\ (n\times 1)} \approx \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} 1 & x_{11} & x_{12} & x_{13}\\ 1 & x_{21} & x_{22} & x_{23}\\ \vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\ 1 & x_{n1} & x_{n2} & x_{n3}\end{pmatrix}}_{\bX\ (n\times(p+1))}\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}\beta_0\\ \beta_1\\ \beta_2\\ \beta_3\end{pmatrix}}_{\bbeta\ ((p+1)\times 1)}\]

Read: the vector of all sales is approximately the design matrix times the coefficient vector. Row \(i\) of \(\bX\bbeta\) is \(1\cdot\beta_0 + x_{i1}\beta_1 + x_{i2}\beta_2 + x_{i3}\beta_3\): exactly the prediction for market \(i\).

SymbolSay it as / meaningTiny example
\(n\)"n": number of rows (markets)\(n=200\)
\(p\)"p": number of predictors (not counting the intercept)\(p=3\)
\(x_{ij}\)"x sub i j": predictor \(j\) for market \(i\)\(x_{12}=37.8\) (radio, market 1)
\(\bX\bbeta\)"X beta": every prediction at oncea column of 200 numbers
\(\hat\bbeta\)"beta-hat": the coefficients we estimate from data\(\hat\beta_1=0.0458\)

Why the column of ones? Matrix multiplication only ever multiplies a column by a coefficient and adds. The intercept \(\beta_0\) is not multiplied by any data value. The trick is to invent a fake predictor that is always 1: then \(1\times\beta_0=\beta_0\) slides into the same multiply-and-add pattern as everything else. Forget the ones and your plane is forced through the origin, which for Advertising would say "zero budget means zero sales". That is false: the intercept turns out to be about 2.9 thousand units.

Step 2 · Closed form: the normal equations

Least squares chooses \(\bbeta\) to make the residual sum of squares as small as possible. In vector form the residuals are \(\by-\bX\bbeta\), and the sum of their squares is the dot product of that vector with itself:

RSS in matrix form
\[\begin{aligned}\RSS(\bbeta)&=\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i-\hat y_i)^2\\&=(\by-\bX\bbeta)\T(\by-\bX\bbeta)\end{aligned}\]

Read: RSS of beta equals (y minus X beta) transpose times (y minus X beta). A row vector times the same column vector multiplies each entry by itself and adds: a sum of squares. Tiny example: residuals \((0.035,-2.685,-3.74)\) give \(0.0012+7.209+13.988=21.198\).

Deriving the normal equations, every step

At the bottom of a smooth bowl the slope is zero in every direction. So we take the gradient of RSS with respect to \(\bbeta\) (the vector of its partial derivatives, taught in Derivatives & gradients) and set it to zero.

Derivation · from RSS to \(\bX\T\bX\hat\bbeta=\bX\T\by\)
\(\RSS(\bbeta)=(\by-\bX\bbeta)\T(\by-\bX\bbeta)\). We want the \(\bbeta\) where \(\nabla_{\bbeta}\RSS=\mathbf 0\).
  1. Multiply out the brackets, like \((a-b)^2=a^2-2ab+b^2\): \[\RSS=\by\T\by-\by\T\bX\bbeta-\bbeta\T\bX\T\by+\bbeta\T\bX\T\bX\bbeta.\]The transpose of a product reverses the order: \((\bX\bbeta)\T=\bbeta\T\bX\T\).
  2. The two middle terms are the same single number (a \(1\times1\) matrix equals its own transpose), so \[\RSS=\by\T\by-2\,\bbeta\T\bX\T\by+\bbeta\T\bX\T\bX\bbeta.\]
  3. Differentiate term by term with two rules from the maths lesson: \(\nabla_{\bbeta}(\bbeta\T\mathbf a)=\mathbf a\), and \(\nabla_{\bbeta}(\bbeta\T A\bbeta)=2A\bbeta\) when \(A\) is symmetric. \(\by\T\by\) has no \(\bbeta\) in it, so its derivative is 0: \[\nabla_{\bbeta}\RSS=-2\bX\T\by+2\bX\T\bX\bbeta.\]These are the vector versions of \(\frac{d}{db}(ab)=a\) and \(\frac{d}{db}(ab^2)=2ab\).
  4. Set it to zero and divide by 2: \(\bX\T\bX\hat\bbeta=\bX\T\by\). These are the normal equations. The hat says "the particular \(\bbeta\) that solves them: our estimate".
  5. Rearranged: \(\bX\T(\by-\bX\hat\bbeta)=\mathbf 0\). Every column of \(\bX\) is perpendicular ("normal") to the residual vector. That is where the name comes from.
\(\bX\T\bX\,\hat\bbeta=\bX\T\by\)
The normal equations
\[\bX\T\bX\,\hat\bbeta=\bX\T\by\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \hat\bbeta=(\bX\T\bX)^{-1}\bX\T\by\ \ \text{(on paper only)}\]

Read: X-transpose-X times beta-hat equals X-transpose-y. It is a square system of \(p+1\) equations in \(p+1\) unknowns: for Advertising, 4 equations in 4 unknowns.

Worked example · solving the normal equations by hand
Three points: \(x=(1,2,3)\), \(y=(1,3,4)\). Fit \(y\approx\beta_0+\beta_1x\).
  1. Build \(\bX\) and \(\by\): \(\bX=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&2\\1&3\end{pmatrix},\ \by=\begin{pmatrix}1\\3\\4\end{pmatrix}\).
  2. \(\bX\T\bX=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1\\1&2&3\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&2\\1&3\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{3}&\boldsymbol{6}\\\boldsymbol{6}&\boldsymbol{14}\end{pmatrix}\).Top-left: \(1+1+1=3\) (the number of points). Off-diagonal: \(1+2+3=6\) (the sum of \(x\)). Bottom-right: \(1+4+9=14\) (the sum of \(x^2\)).
  3. \(\bX\T\by=\begin{pmatrix}1+3+4\\ 1(1)+2(3)+3(4)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}8\\19\end{pmatrix}\).The sum of \(y\), and the sum of \(xy\).
  4. Solve \(\begin{pmatrix}3&6\\6&14\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\beta_0\\\beta_1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}8\\19\end{pmatrix}\). Double the first equation: \(6\beta_0+12\beta_1=16\). Subtract it from the second: \(2\beta_1=3\), so \(\beta_1=\mathbf{1.5}\).This is Gaussian elimination: what np.linalg.solve does, with extra care about rounding.
  5. Back-substitute into the first equation: \(3\beta_0+6(1.5)=8\Rightarrow 3\beta_0=-1\Rightarrow\beta_0=\mathbf{-0.3333}\).
  6. Check perpendicularity. Predictions \(-0.3333+1.5x=(1.1667,\ 2.6667,\ 4.1667)\); residuals \((-0.1667,\ 0.3333,\ -0.1667)\). Their sum is \(0\), and \(1(-0.1667)+2(0.3333)+3(-0.1667)=0\). Both columns of \(\bX\) are perpendicular to the residuals, as promised.
\(\hat\beta_0=-1/3,\ \hat\beta_1=3/2\), RSS \(=0.0278+0.1111+0.0278=0.1667\)

You have seen these equations before. In adaptive filtering, the optimal (Wiener) filter solves the Wiener–Hopf equations \(R\,\mathbf w=\mathbf p\), where \(R\) is the autocorrelation matrix of the input and \(\mathbf p\) the cross-correlation between the input and the desired signal. Divide the normal equations by \(n\): \(\tfrac1n\bX\T\bX\) is the sample autocorrelation of the inputs and \(\tfrac1n\bX\T\by\) the sample cross-correlation. Least squares is the Wiener filter with sample averages, and the gradient descent you write in Step 5 is the steepest-descent algorithm that LMS approximates.

Why solve and never inv

On paper we write \((\bX\T\bX)^{-1}\). On a computer you should almost never form an inverse. np.linalg.solve(A, b) finds the \(\mathbf x\) with \(A\mathbf x=\mathbf b\) directly by elimination (an LU factorisation). Computing inv(A) @ b does more work and, more importantly, loses more accuracy when \(A\) is nearly singular. "Nearly singular" is measured by the condition number \(\kappa(A)\) (read kappa of A), the ratio of the largest to the smallest stretch \(A\) applies to a vector. A rough rule: you can lose about \(\log_{10}\kappa\) of your 16 decimal digits. For Advertising, \(\kappa(\bX\T\bX)\approx 2\times10^5\), so about 5 digits are at risk, which is still fine. With polynomial features it gets far worse, as the cell after the next exercise shows.

Read the output: the condition number is about \(10^{16}\): every one of our 16 digits is at risk. The inverse leaves a residual of order 1 to 10 in its own equations and misses the true coefficients badly. solve satisfies its equations almost perfectly (about \(10^{-14}\)), but still misses the true coefficients by order 1, because the problem itself is too sensitive. lstsq, which never squares the condition number by forming \(V\T V\), recovers them to about \(10^{-9}\). (At this condition number the last digits depend on your processor, so your exact numbers may differ a little from the reference output; the ranking does not.) Lesson: solve beats inv; for ill-conditioned problems lstsq beats both. For the well-behaved Advertising problem all three agree to many digits.

Interview favourite. Code like inv(X.T @ X) @ X.T @ y invites the question "why?". The answer they want: "inverting is slower and less numerically stable; I would use solve, or lstsq/QR when the design matrix might be ill-conditioned".

Step 4 · The gradient of the loss

The closed form needs a square system you can solve. Neural networks have no such formula: they are trained by walking downhill on the loss. Linear regression is the perfect place to learn the walk, because we know exactly where the bottom is and can check that we arrive.

We use the mean squared error (MSE) as the loss. It is RSS divided by \(n\). Dividing by \(n\) does not move the minimum, but it keeps the numbers a sensible size whatever \(n\) is:

Loss and gradient for linear regression
\[L(\bbeta)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\big(y_i-\bx_i\T\bbeta\big)^2=\frac1n\|\by-\bX\bbeta\|^2,\qquad \nabla L(\bbeta)=-\frac2n\,\bX\T(\by-\bX\bbeta)\]

Read: the loss is the average squared residual. Its gradient is minus two over n, times X-transpose, times the residual vector. Entry \(j\) of the gradient is \(-\frac2n\sum_i x_{ij}\,r_i\): "how strongly predictor \(j\) lines up with what we are still getting wrong".

Derivation for one coefficient, with the chain rule from Derivatives & gradients. Write the residual \(r_i=y_i-\sum_k x_{ik}\beta_k\). Only the \(k=j\) term contains \(\beta_j\), so \(\partial r_i/\partial\beta_j=-x_{ij}\). Then

\[\frac{\partial L}{\partial\beta_j}=\frac1n\sum_i 2r_i\cdot\frac{\partial r_i}{\partial\beta_j}=\frac1n\sum_i 2r_i(-x_{ij})=-\frac2n\sum_i x_{ij}r_i.\]

Stacking the \(p+1\) entries gives \(-\frac2n\bX\T\mathbf r\). Setting it to zero gives back the normal equations: the two methods are aiming at the same point.

Step 6 · Feature scaling and the learning rate

Standardising a column means subtracting its mean and dividing by its standard deviation, so that every predictor is measured "in standard deviations from average":

Standardisation (z-scores)
\[z_{ij}=\frac{x_{ij}-m_j}{s_j},\qquad m_j=\frac1n\sum_i x_{ij},\quad s_j=\sqrt{\frac1n\sum_i(x_{ij}-m_j)^2}\]

Read: z sub i j is x sub i j minus the column mean, divided by the column standard deviation. A TV budget of 233 with \(m=147.04\) and \(s=85.64\) becomes \((233-147.04)/85.64=1.00\): one standard deviation above average.

Why does this help? For the MSE, the shape of the loss bowl is set by the matrix \(H=\frac2n\bX\T\bX\), the Hessian (the matrix of second derivatives: how fast the slope changes). Its eigenvalues are the curvatures along the bowl's main directions. Two facts follow:

  • Gradient descent is stable only if \(\eta\lt 2/\lambda_{\max}\) (read lambda max, the largest curvature). Above that, each step overshoots further than the last.
  • Along the flattest direction (curvature \(\lambda_{\min}\)) each step removes only a fraction \(\eta\lambda_{\min}\) of the remaining error. With \(\eta\) close to \(2/\lambda_{\max}\), that fraction is about \(2\lambda_{\min}/\lambda_{\max}\).

So the number of steps needed grows like \(\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}\), the condition number. Here are the actual numbers for Advertising (you will compute them in the next cell):

Step 8 · Diagnostic plots: is a straight plane good enough?

A single number such as test MSE tells you how big the errors are, not what shape they have. Diagnostic plots show the shape. The most useful one is residuals against fitted values: for each market, put \(\hat y_i\) on the x-axis and \(r_i=y_i-\hat y_i\) on the y-axis. If the model has captured everything systematic, the dots form a shapeless horizontal band around zero. Any pattern (a curve, a funnel) is something the model missed (ISLP 3.3.3).

We also report \(R^2\), the fraction of the variance of \(y\) that the model explains:

Coefficient of determination
\[R^2=1-\frac{\RSS}{\TSS}=1-\frac{\sum_i(y_i-\hat y_i)^2}{\sum_i(y_i-\bar y)^2}\]

Read: R-squared is one minus the residual sum of squares over the total sum of squares. TSS is the error of the laziest model, "always predict the mean \(\bar y\)". Tiny example: \(y=(1,3,4)\), \(\bar y=2.667\), TSS \(=2.778+0.111+1.778=4.667\); with RSS \(=0.1667\), \(R^2=1-0.1667/4.667=0.964\).

RSS is the sum of squared residuals; TSS is the sum of squared distances from y_true.mean().

rss = np.sum((y_true - y_pred) ** 2) and tss = np.sum((y_true - y_true.mean()) ** 2).

def r2(y_true, y_pred):
    y_true, y_pred = np.asarray(y_true), np.asarray(y_pred)
    rss = np.sum((y_true - y_pred) ** 2)
    tss = np.sum((y_true - y_true.mean()) ** 2)
    return 1 - rss / tss

Why does the design matrix have a column of ones?

  1. To make \(\bX\T\bX\) invertible
  2. So that the intercept \(\beta_0\) fits the same multiply-and-add pattern as the slopes
  3. To standardise the data
  4. NumPy requires it
Row \(i\) of \(\bX\bbeta\) is \(\sum_j x_{ij}\beta_j\); a constant-1 column makes the term \(1\cdot\beta_0\) appear. It does not help invertibility (it can even hurt it), it has nothing to do with scaling, and NumPy does not care.

The normal equations say \(\bX\T(\by-\bX\hat\bbeta)=\mathbf 0\). In words:

  1. The residuals are all zero
  2. The predictions equal the data
  3. Every column of \(\bX\) is perpendicular to the residual vector
  4. \(\bX\) is an orthogonal matrix
Each entry of \(\bX\T\mathbf r\) is the dot product of one column with the residuals. The residuals themselves are not zero (RSS is 556.8 for Advertising). Because the ones column is one of the columns, the residuals also sum to zero.

Why prefer np.linalg.solve(A, b) over np.linalg.inv(A) @ b?

  1. They give different answers in exact arithmetic
  2. solve is faster and loses less accuracy when \(A\) is ill-conditioned
  3. inv does not work for symmetric matrices
  4. solve works for non-square \(A\)
In exact arithmetic they are equal. In floating point, forming the inverse costs more and amplifies rounding errors (in the polynomial test, a residual about \(10^{14}\) times larger than solve's). Both need a square \(A\); for non-square problems use lstsq.

For \(x=(1,2,3)\), \(y=(1,3,4)\), at \(\bbeta=(0,0)\), what is \(\partial L/\partial\beta_1\) for the MSE loss?

\(-\frac23\sum_i x_i r_i=-\frac23(1\cdot1+2\cdot3+3\cdot4)=-\frac23\times19=-12.667\).

The Hessian of the scaled problem has largest eigenvalue 2.74. Above what learning rate does gradient descent diverge? (2 decimals)

It is stable only if \(\eta\lt 2/\lambda_{\max}=2/2.74=0.73\). The cell with lr = 0.75 diverged, as predicted.

You standardise all 200 rows, then split into train and test. What is wrong?

  1. The test rows influenced the scaling used in training: a (small) leak
  2. Nothing: scaling is not fitting
  3. The coefficients become biased
  4. Gradient descent will diverge
Means and standard deviations are statistics estimated from data. Computing them with the test rows lets the test set shape training. Compute them on the training rows and reuse them for the test rows.

  1. lower the test MSE
  2. raise the test MSE
  3. raise the training MSE
  4. leave the training MSE the same or lower it
Least squares on more columns can always reproduce the smaller model (coefficient 0), so training RSS cannot rise. Test error can go either way; here it rose slightly, from 1.399 to 1.404.

  1. The model is overfitting
  2. The learning rate was too high
  3. The model misses a systematic non-linear pattern (here, a TV × radio interaction)

Explain to a friend, with no formulas, why gradient descent is slow on raw Advertising budgets and fast after standardising.

The loss is a valley. With raw budgets the TV column has huge numbers and the ones column tiny ones, so the valley is extremely steep across and almost flat along its floor. The step size must be small enough not to bounce out across the steep sides, and then the steps along the flat floor are tiny, so you crawl. Standardising puts every column on the same scale, the valley becomes a round bowl, and one step size works well in every direction.
The normal equations
\(\bX\T\bX\hat\bbeta=\bX\T\by\)
Gradient of \(L=\frac1n\|\by-\bX\bbeta\|^2\)
\(\nabla L=-\frac2n\bX\T(\by-\bX\bbeta)\)
Gradient descent update
\(\bbeta\leftarrow\bbeta-\eta\nabla L(\bbeta)\)
Why the ones column in \(\bX\)?
It carries the intercept: \(1\cdot\beta_0\).
Why solve, not inv?
Fewer operations, and less loss of accuracy on ill-conditioned systems.
Stability limit for gradient descent on a quadratic loss
\(\eta\lt 2/\lambda_{\max}\) of the Hessian \(\frac2n\bX\T\bX\).
What sets how slowly GD converges on a quadratic?
The condition number \(\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}\) of the Hessian.
Standardised feature
\(z=(x-m)/s\), with \(m,s\) from the training rows.
Convert standardised coefficients back
\(\beta_j=b_j/s_j\), \(\beta_0=b_0-\sum_j\beta_jm_j\).
Centred finite difference
\(\frac{f(\beta+h)-f(\beta-h)}{2h}\); error shrinks like \(h^2\).
