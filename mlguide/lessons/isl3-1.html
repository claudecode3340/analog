Simple linear regression fits a straight line through a cloud of points and then tells you how much to trust that line. Nearly every model you will meet later, from multiple regression to neural networks, reuses its ideas: residuals, least squares, standard errors, tests and R². Learn it here with every number shown, and the rest of machine learning gets easier.

Why we are here: seven questions about advertising

Chapter 3 is built around one story. You are a consultant. A company gives you the Advertising data: for 200 markets, the money spent on TV, radio and newspaper advertising (in thousands of dollars) and the product's sales (in thousands of units). They want a marketing plan for next year. Before you can advise them, the book says you need answers to seven questions:

#QuestionTool that answers it (where)
1Is there a relationship between advertising budget and sales?t-test (this lesson), F-test (3.2)
2How strong is the relationship?RSE and \(R^2\) (this lesson)
3Which media are associated with sales?multiple regression p-values (3.2)
4How large is the association between each medium and sales?confidence intervals (this lesson and 3.2)
5How accurately can we predict future sales?prediction intervals (3.2)

“Synergy” is the marketing word for what statisticians call an interaction: spending $50,000 on TV and $50,000 on radio might sell more than $100,000 on either one alone. Keep these seven questions in mind. By the end of lesson 3.3 you will have answered every one of them with numbers.

In this lesson we use only one predictor, the TV budget, and ask: what straight line best describes how sales change with TV spending, and how sure can we be about it?

The model: a straight line plus noise

In plain words: we guess that sales go up (or down) by roughly the same amount for every extra thousand dollars of TV advertising. A straight line says exactly that. The book writes it as

The simple linear regression model (3.1)
\[Y \approx \beta_0 + \beta_1 X\]

Read: “Y is approximately beta-nought plus beta-one times X.” The wavy equals sign \(\approx\) means “is approximately modelled as”.

We say we are regressing \(Y\) on \(X\) (or “onto \(X\)”). For our data: \(\text{sales} \approx \beta_0 + \beta_1\times\text{TV}\).

SymbolSay it as / meaningTiny example
\(X\)“X”: the predictor (input), here the TV budget in thousands of dollars\(X=100\) means $100,000 on TV
\(Y\)“Y”: the response (output), here sales in thousands of units\(Y=12\) means 12,000 units sold
\(\beta_0\)“beta-nought” (or beta-zero): the intercept, the height of the line when \(X=0\)\(\beta_0=7\): with no TV ads, about 7,000 units
\(\beta_1\)“beta-one”: the slope, how much \(Y\) changes when \(X\) goes up by 1\(\beta_1=0.05\): +1 in TV gives +0.05 in sales
\(\hat\beta_0,\ \hat\beta_1\)“beta-nought-hat”, “beta-one-hat”: our estimates of \(\beta_0,\beta_1\) from data. A hat always means “estimated”\(\hat\beta_1=0.0475\)
\(\hat y\)“y-hat”: our prediction of \(Y\)\(\hat y = 7.03+0.0475\times100=11.78\)

Together \(\beta_0\) and \(\beta_1\) are called the model coefficients or parameters. They are unknown. We use training data to compute estimates \(\hat\beta_0,\hat\beta_1\), and then predict with

Prediction (3.2)
\[\hat y = \hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1 x\]

Read: “y-hat equals beta-nought-hat plus beta-one-hat times x.” Put in a TV budget \(x\), get out a predicted sales figure.

Tiny example. With the book's estimates \(\hat\beta_0=7.03\) and \(\hat\beta_1=0.0475\), a market that spends \(x=200\) (that is, $200,000) on TV is predicted to sell \(\hat y = 7.03 + 0.0475\times 200 = 7.03+9.50=16.53\) thousand units.

Least squares: what “best line” means

Many lines pass near the points. We need a rule to choose one. Suppose we have \(n\) pairs of observations \((x_1,y_1),(x_2,y_2),\dots,(x_n,y_n)\); for Advertising, \(n=200\) markets. For any candidate line, the prediction for market \(i\) is \(\hat y_i=\hat\beta_0+\hat\beta_1x_i\). The miss for that market is the residual:

Residual
\[e_i = y_i - \hat y_i\]

Read: “e-sub-i equals y-sub-i minus y-hat-sub-i”: what really happened minus what the line predicted. Positive means the point is above the line.

We add up the squared residuals to get one number that measures how badly the line fits, the residual sum of squares (RSS):

RSS (3.3)
\[\RSS = e_1^2+e_2^2+\dots+e_n^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n \big(y_i-\hat\beta_0-\hat\beta_1x_i\big)^2\]

Read: “RSS is the sum, over every observation i, of (y-i minus beta-nought-hat minus beta-one-hat x-i) squared.”

The least squares line is the one whose \(\hat\beta_0,\hat\beta_1\) make RSS as small as possible.

Tiny example. Three points \((1,2),(2,3),(3,5)\) and the line \(\hat y = 0.5+1.5x\). Predictions: \(2,\ 3.5,\ 5\). Residuals: \(2-2=0\), \(3-3.5=-0.5\), \(5-5=0\). RSS \(=0^2+(-0.5)^2+0^2=0.25\).

Why square the residuals? Three reasons. (1) Squaring makes every miss positive, so misses above and below the line cannot cancel. (2) Big misses are punished much more than small ones: a miss of 4 costs 16, a miss of 1 costs 1. (3) Squares are smooth, so calculus gives an exact formula for the best line. Other choices exist (Chapter 6 discusses some), but least squares is by far the most common.

You have met this before. Minimising a sum of squared errors is minimising the energy of an error signal. The Wiener filter, LMS adaptive equalisers and least-squares channel estimation all minimise exactly this kind of quantity. Linear regression is the simplest member of that family: a “filter” with two taps, \(\beta_0\) and \(\beta_1\).

Deriving the least-squares formulas, step by step

The book says “using some calculus, one can show” the answer. Here is that calculus with nothing skipped. It uses exactly the recipe from Finding a minimum and Partial derivatives: at the bottom of a smooth bowl, the slope is zero in every direction.

Treat RSS as a function of two unknowns, \(b_0\) and \(b_1\) (we drop the hats while we search):

\[\RSS(b_0,b_1)=\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i-b_0-b_1x_i)^2 .\]
Derivation · the least-squares estimates (3.4)
Minimise \(\RSS(b_0,b_1)=\sum_i (y_i-b_0-b_1x_i)^2\). Notation: \(\bar x=\frac1n\sum_i x_i\) (read “x-bar”, the mean of the \(x\)'s) and \(\bar y=\frac1n\sum_i y_i\).
  1. Differentiate with respect to \(b_0\). Chain rule on each term: the derivative of \((\text{stuff})^2\) is \(2(\text{stuff})\) times the derivative of the stuff, and the derivative of \(y_i-b_0-b_1x_i\) with respect to \(b_0\) is \(-1\): \[\frac{\partial \RSS}{\partial b_0}=\sum_i 2(y_i-b_0-b_1x_i)(-1)=-2\sum_i (y_i-b_0-b_1x_i).\]\(\partial\) is read “partial”: differentiate with respect to \(b_0\) while holding \(b_1\) fixed.
  2. Set it to zero and solve for \(b_0\). Divide by \(-2\): \(\sum_i y_i - n b_0 - b_1\sum_i x_i = 0\). (Adding \(b_0\) to itself \(n\) times gives \(nb_0\).) Divide by \(n\): \[\bar y - b_0 - b_1\bar x = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{b_0=\bar y-b_1\bar x}.\]This already tells us something lovely: the best line passes through the point \((\bar x,\bar y)\). Put \(x=\bar x\) into the line and you get \(\bar y\).
  3. Differentiate with respect to \(b_1\). Now the inner derivative is \(-x_i\): \[\frac{\partial \RSS}{\partial b_1}=\sum_i 2(y_i-b_0-b_1x_i)(-x_i)=-2\sum_i x_i(y_i-b_0-b_1x_i).\]
  4. Substitute \(b_0=\bar y-b_1\bar x\) and set to zero: \[\sum_i x_i\big(y_i-\bar y+b_1\bar x-b_1x_i\big)=0\ \Longrightarrow\ \sum_i x_i(y_i-\bar y) - b_1\sum_i x_i(x_i-\bar x)=0.\]
  5. Solve for \(b_1\): \[b_1=\frac{\sum_i x_i(y_i-\bar y)}{\sum_i x_i(x_i-\bar x)}.\]
  6. Make it symmetric. Deviations from a mean add to zero: \(\sum_i (y_i-\bar y)=0\) and \(\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)=0\). So we may subtract \(\bar x\sum_i(y_i-\bar y)=0\) from the top and \(\bar x\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)=0\) from the bottom without changing anything: \[\sum_i x_i(y_i-\bar y)=\sum_i (x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y),\qquad \sum_i x_i(x_i-\bar x)=\sum_i (x_i-\bar x)^2.\]
  7. Is it really a minimum? The second derivatives are \(\partial^2\RSS/\partial b_0^2=2n>0\), \(\partial^2\RSS/\partial b_1^2=2\sum x_i^2>0\) and \(\partial^2\RSS/\partial b_0\partial b_1=2\sum x_i\). The determinant of this 2 × 2 matrix is \(4\big(n\sum x_i^2-(\sum x_i)^2\big)=4n\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2>0\) as long as the \(x_i\) are not all equal. So RSS is a bowl that opens upwards (it is convex), and the flat point is the bottom.If all \(x_i\) are equal, \(\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2=0\) and no unique slope exists: you cannot learn a slope from points stacked on one vertical line.
\(\hat\beta_1=\dfrac{\sum_i (x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)}{\sum_i (x_i-\bar x)^2},\qquad \hat\beta_0=\bar y-\hat\beta_1\bar x\)
Least-squares coefficient estimates (3.4)
\[\hat\beta_1=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)}{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i-\bar x)^2},\qquad \hat\beta_0=\bar y-\hat\beta_1\bar x\]

Read: “beta-one-hat is the sum of (x-deviation times y-deviation) divided by the sum of squared x-deviations. Beta-nought-hat is y-bar minus beta-one-hat times x-bar.” The top measures how \(x\) and \(y\) move together; the bottom measures how much \(x\) moves on its own.

We will write \(S_{xy}=\sum(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)\) and \(S_{xx}=\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2\) as short names, so \(\hat\beta_1=S_{xy}/S_{xx}\). (If you divide top and bottom by \(n-1\), \(\hat\beta_1\) is the sample covariance of \(x\) and \(y\) divided by the sample variance of \(x\).)

The RSS surface: a bowl with one bottom

RSS is a function of two numbers, \(\beta_0\) and \(\beta_1\), so we can draw it as a landscape over the \((\beta_0,\beta_1)\) plane. Each residual is linear in \(\beta_0\) and \(\beta_1\), so RSS, a sum of their squares, is a quadratic function of them: a smooth bowl. Its contours (lines of equal height, like on a map) are ellipses.

The line starts too steep and too low. Move the two sliders (or click on the contour map) and watch the red residual segments and the RSS. Try to get the RSS below 2,150 by hand, then press Jump to least squares. Moving along the long axis of the tilted ellipses changes RSS slowly; moving across them changes it fast.

Population line versus least-squares line

So far we have fitted a line to the data we have. Now step back. Why should the line fitted to these 200 markets tell us anything about markets we have not seen? To answer, the book writes down a model for how the data were generated:

The true (population) model (3.5)
\[Y=\beta_0+\beta_1X+\epsilon\]

Read: “Y equals beta-nought plus beta-one X plus epsilon.” \(\epsilon\) (epsilon) is the error term: a random amount, with mean zero, added to every observation.

The error term is a catch-all for everything the straight line misses: the true relationship is probably not exactly linear, other variables also affect \(Y\), and there is measurement error. We usually assume \(\epsilon\) is independent of \(X\). Here \(\beta_0\) is the expected value of \(Y\) when \(X=0\), and \(\beta_1\) is the average increase in \(Y\) for a one-unit increase in \(X\).

The line \(\beta_0+\beta_1X\) with the true, unknown coefficients is the population regression line: the best linear approximation to the truth. The line \(\hat\beta_0+\hat\beta_1X\) computed from our data is the least-squares line. They are different lines, and we only ever see the second one.

This is the same situation as estimating a mean, which you met in Samples and the standard error. The population mean \(\mu\) is unknown; the sample mean \(\hat\mu=\bar y\) estimates it; a different sample gives a different \(\bar y\), but \(\bar y\) is unbiased: averaged over a huge number of samples, it equals \(\mu\). The least-squares estimates are unbiased too: \(\E(\hat\beta_0)=\beta_0\) and \(\E(\hat\beta_1)=\beta_1\). A single estimate may be too high or too low, but there is no systematic lean.

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)              # a seeded random number generator
slopes = []
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6, 4))
for k in range(10):                          # ten data sets, as in Figure 3.3 (right)
    xs = rng.uniform(-2, 2, size=100)

    slopes.append(s)

ax.plot([-2, 2], [-4, 8], color='#C0392F', lw=2.5, label='population line 2 + 3X')
ax.legend(); ax.set_xlabel('X'); ax.set_ylabel('Y')

print("average of the ten slopes:", round(np.mean(slopes), 3))

Standard errors: how far off is one estimate?

Unbiased means “right on average”. But we get only one data set. How far off might our estimate be? For the sample mean, m-prob gave the answer: its standard error.

Standard error of the sample mean (3.7)
\[\Var(\hat\mu)=\SE(\hat\mu)^2=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\]

Read: “the variance of mu-hat equals SE of mu-hat squared equals sigma squared over n.” \(\sigma\) is the standard deviation of each observation. Roughly, the SE is the typical distance between the estimate and the truth.

Tiny reminder. Heights with \(\sigma=10\) cm and \(n=25\): \(\SE=10/\sqrt{25}=2\) cm; with \(n=100\), \(\SE=1\) cm. (This needs the observations to be uncorrelated.) The regression coefficients have formulas of the same flavour:

Standard errors of \(\hat\beta_0\) and \(\hat\beta_1\) (3.8)
\[\SE(\hat\beta_0)^2=\sigma^2\left[\frac1n+\frac{\bar x^2}{\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\bar x)^2}\right],\qquad \SE(\hat\beta_1)^2=\frac{\sigma^2}{\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\bar x)^2}\]

Read: “SE of beta-one-hat squared is sigma squared divided by the sum of squared x-deviations.” Here \(\sigma^2=\Var(\epsilon)\), the variance of the error term.

Three things to read from these formulas:

  • More noise, bigger SE. \(\sigma\) is on top.
  • More spread-out \(x\), smaller \(\SE(\hat\beta_1)\). \(S_{xx}\) is on the bottom. If your \(x\)'s are bunched together, a little noise can tilt the line a lot; if they are spread out, you have more leverage to pin down the slope, like holding a ruler at its two ends rather than near the middle.
  • If \(\bar x=0\), \(\SE(\hat\beta_0)^2=\sigma^2/n\), the same as for a sample mean. In that case \(\hat\beta_0=\bar y\).

These formulas assume the errors \(\epsilon_i\) all have the same variance \(\sigma^2\) and are uncorrelated. Figure 3.1's fan shape shows this is not quite true for Advertising, but the book notes the formulas are still a good approximation. Lesson 3.3 shows what to do when these assumptions fail.

Estimating \(\sigma\): the residual standard error

We do not know \(\sigma\). We estimate it from the residuals with the residual standard error:

Residual standard error (3.15)
\[\text{RSE}=\sqrt{\frac{\RSS}{n-2}}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n-2}\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\hat y_i)^2}\]

Read: “RSE is the square root of RSS divided by n minus 2.” It is the typical size of a residual.

Why \(n-2\) and not \(n\)? We used the data to estimate two numbers (\(\hat\beta_0\) and \(\hat\beta_1\)), and the residuals are forced to satisfy two equations (\(\sum e_i=0\) and \(\sum x_ie_i=0\)). Only \(n-2\) of them are free: these are the degrees of freedom. It is the same reason the sample variance in m-prob divides by \(n-1\) (one mean estimated). Dividing by \(n-2\) makes \(\text{RSE}^2\) an unbiased estimate of \(\sigma^2\). (Strictly we should write \(\widehat{\SE}\) once \(\sigma\) is replaced by RSE; the book, like everyone, drops the extra hat.)

Confidence intervals

You built confidence intervals for a mean in m-prob. The recipe for a coefficient is identical: estimate ± multiplier × SE. The 95% confidence interval has the property that, if we repeated the whole experiment many times and built the interval each time, 95% of those intervals would contain the true value. For regression it is approximately

Approximate 95% confidence intervals (3.9)–(3.11)
\[\hat\beta_1\pm2\cdot\SE(\hat\beta_1)=\big[\hat\beta_1-2\,\SE(\hat\beta_1),\ \hat\beta_1+2\,\SE(\hat\beta_1)\big],\qquad \hat\beta_0\pm2\cdot\SE(\hat\beta_0)\]

Read: “beta-one-hat plus or minus two standard errors.”

The “2” is a rounding of the exact multiplier \(t_{0.975,\,n-2}\), the 97.5% point of a t-distribution with \(n-2\) degrees of freedom (assuming normal errors): 1.972 for \(n-2=198\), but 3.182 for \(n-2=3\). The t-distribution's heavier tails pay for having estimated \(\sigma\); see the distributions you will meet.

Worked example · the book's intervals for Advertising
\(\hat\beta_0=7.0326\), \(\SE=0.4578\); \(\hat\beta_1=0.047537\), \(\SE=0.0026906\); exact multiplier \(t_{0.975,198}=1.972\).
  1. \(\beta_1\): half-width \(1.972\times0.0026906=0.005306\). Interval \([0.047537-0.005306,\ 0.047537+0.005306]=[0.042,\ 0.053]\).
  2. \(\beta_0\): half-width \(1.972\times0.4578=0.9029\). Interval \([7.0326-0.9029,\ 7.0326+0.9029]=[6.130,\ 7.935]\).
  3. In words. With no TV advertising, average sales are somewhere between 6,130 and 7,935 units. Each extra $1,000 of TV advertising is associated with an average increase of between 42 and 53 units.

Pitfall: what “95% confidence” does not mean. It does not mean “there is a 95% probability that \(\beta_1\) is in this interval”: \(\beta_1\) is a fixed number, and it either is or is not inside. The 95% describes the procedure: over many repeated data sets, 95% of intervals built this way catch the true value.

Hypothesis tests, t-statistics and p-values

Question 1 of our seven was: is there any relationship between TV and sales at all? As in m-prob, we turn it into a hypothesis test. The null hypothesis \(H_0\) is the boring explanation; the alternative \(H_a\) is the interesting one:

Testing for a relationship (3.12)–(3.13)
\[H_0:\ \beta_1=0\quad\text{(no relationship)}\qquad\text{versus}\qquad H_a:\ \beta_1\neq0\quad\text{(some relationship)}\]

Read: “H-nought: beta-one equals zero, versus H-a: beta-one is not zero.” If \(\beta_1=0\) the model becomes \(Y=\beta_0+\epsilon\) and \(X\) plays no part.

Our \(\hat\beta_1=0.0475\) is not zero, but it never would be exactly zero, even with no real relationship, because of noise. Is it far enough from zero? “Far” must be measured in standard errors. That is the t-statistic:

t-statistic (3.14)
\[t=\frac{\hat\beta_1-0}{\SE(\hat\beta_1)}\]

Read: “t equals beta-one-hat minus zero, over its standard error”: how many standard errors the estimate is away from zero.

If \(H_0\) is true, \(t\) follows a t-distribution with \(n-2\) degrees of freedom. The p-value is the probability of seeing \(|t|\) at least as large as ours if \(H_0\) were true. A small p-value says “a slope this far from zero would be very surprising if there were no relationship”, so we reject \(H_0\). Typical cut-offs are 5% or 1%; for \(n=30\) these correspond to \(|t|\) of about 2 and 2.75.

Worked example · t and p for the five points and for Advertising

How well does the line fit? RSE and R²

Once we know there is a relationship (question 1), we ask how strong it is (question 2). The book reports three numbers in Table 3.2:

Table 3.2 (ISLP p. 78). More information about the least-squares model for the regression of units sold on TV budget.

RSE: lack of fit in the units of \(Y\)

The RSE (3.15) estimates the standard deviation of \(\epsilon\): roughly, how far the response typically lands from the true line. Advertising's RSE is 3.26, so even if we knew the true \(\beta_0\) and \(\beta_1\) exactly, a prediction of a market's sales from TV alone would typically be off by about 3,260 units. Is that a lot? Average sales are about 14,000 units, so the percentage error is \(3{,}260/14{,}000\approx23\%\). RSS itself is (3.16): \(\RSS=\sum(y_i-\hat y_i)^2\).

R²: the fraction of variance explained

RSE has units, so it is hard to say what a “good” RSE is. \(R^2\) is unit-free and always between 0 and 1. It compares two sums of squares:

  • the total sum of squares \(\text{TSS}=\sum(y_i-\bar y)^2\): how much \(y\) varies before regression, when the best guess for every market is just \(\bar y\);
  • the RSS: how much variation is left after regression.
The \(R^2\) statistic (3.17)
\[R^2=\frac{\text{TSS}-\RSS}{\text{TSS}}=1-\frac{\RSS}{\text{TSS}}\]

Read: “R-squared equals TSS minus RSS, over TSS”: the proportion of the variability in \(Y\) that is explained by \(X\).

Five-point example: \(\text{TSS}=6\), \(\RSS=2.4\), so \(R^2=1-2.4/6=1-0.4=0.6\). The line explains 60% of the variation in sales.

Advertising: \(\text{TSS}=5417.15\), \(\RSS=2102.53\), so \(R^2=1-2102.53/5417.15=1-0.3881=0.612\). Just under two-thirds of the variability in sales is explained by a linear regression on TV.

What counts as a good \(R^2\) depends on the field. In a physics experiment where the law really is linear, anything below 0.99 might mean a broken instrument. In biology, psychology or marketing, where many unmeasured factors matter, an \(R^2\) well below 0.1 can be realistic and still useful.

Correlation, and why \(R^2=r^2\) here

The sample correlation between \(X\) and \(Y\) (see m-prob) also measures a linear relationship:

Correlation (3.18)
\[r=\text{Cor}(X,Y)=\frac{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)}{\sqrt{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2}\sqrt{\sum_i(y_i-\bar y)^2}}=\frac{S_{xy}}{\sqrt{S_{xx}\,\text{TSS}}}\]

Read: “r is S-x-y over the square root of S-x-x times S-y-y.” It lies between −1 and +1.

Five points: \(r=6/\sqrt{10\times6}=6/7.746=0.7746\), and \(r^2=0.6=R^2\). Advertising: \(r=69{,}727.65/\sqrt{1{,}466{,}819\times5417.15}=0.7822\), and \(0.7822^2=0.612=R^2\). In simple linear regression \(R^2=r^2\) always (you will prove it in Exercise 7). With several predictors correlation no longer works, because it relates only two variables at a time, and \(R^2\) takes over.

And the F-statistic in Table 3.2? It belongs to lesson 3.2. With one predictor it is simply \(t^2\): \(17.67^2=312.1\).

Pitfall: \(R^2\) measures linear fit only. A perfect U-shaped relationship can have \(R^2\) near 0. And a high \(R^2\) does not make a model correct: Figure 3.1 has \(R^2=0.61\) yet the residuals fan out, which breaks the equal-variance assumption behind the standard errors. Always look at the plot.

  3. It is a squared quantity
A hat always marks an estimate (or a prediction). The true slope \(\beta_1\) has no hat and is unknown. Nothing is squared.

Data: \(x=(0,2,4)\), \(y=(1,1,3)\). Compute \(\hat\beta_1\).

\(\bar x=2\), \(\bar y=5/3\). Deviations: \(x\): −2, 0, 2; \(y\): −2/3, −2/3, 4/3. \(S_{xy}=(−2)(−2/3)+0+2(4/3)=4/3+8/3=4\). \(S_{xx}=4+0+4=8\). \(\hat\beta_1=4/8=0.5\).

Same data, \(x=(0,2,4)\), \(y=(1,1,3)\), \(\hat\beta_1=0.5\). Compute \(\hat\beta_0\) (to 3 decimals).

\(\hat\beta_0=\bar y-\hat\beta_1\bar x=5/3-0.5\times2=1.667-1=0.667\).

You collect the same number of points, but your \(x\) values are spread over a range three times as wide. Noise is unchanged. What happens to \(\SE(\hat\beta_1)\)?

  1. It triples
  2. It stays the same
  3. It shrinks to about a third
Tripling the spread multiplies every \(x_i-\bar x\) by 3, so \(S_{xx}\) is multiplied by 9 and \(\SE(\hat\beta_1)=\sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}\) is divided by 3. Spread-out inputs pin down a slope better.

A slope has \(\hat\beta_1=0.8\) and \(\SE(\hat\beta_1)=0.5\), with \(n=200\). Which statement is right?

  1. \(t=1.6\); we cannot reject \(H_0:\beta_1=0\) at the 5% level
  2. \(t=1.6\); we reject \(H_0\) because 0.8 is not zero
  3. \(t=0.4\); we cannot reject \(H_0\)
\(t=0.8/0.5=1.6\), below the cut-off of about 1.97, so p ≈ 0.11. Being non-zero is not enough: the estimate must be far from zero in units of its standard error. Option C divides the wrong way round.

A regression has TSS = 400 and RSS = 100. What is \(R^2\)?

\(R^2=1-\RSS/\text{TSS}=1-100/400=0.75\): 75% of the variance is explained.

In a simple regression, the correlation between \(X\) and \(Y\) is \(r=-0.9\). What is \(R^2\)?

  1. −0.81
  2. 0.81
  3. 0.9
\(R^2=r^2=(-0.9)^2=0.81\). \(R^2\) can never be negative for least squares with an intercept; the sign of \(r\) gives the direction of the slope, not the strength.

Why does the RSE divide RSS by \(n-2\) instead of \(n\)?

  1. To make the RSE look smaller
  2. Because two observations are always outliers
  3. Because two parameters were estimated from the same data, leaving \(n-2\) degrees of freedom
Fitting \(\hat\beta_0,\hat\beta_1\) forces two constraints on the residuals (\(\sum e_i=0\), \(\sum x_ie_i=0\)), so the residuals are a little smaller than the true errors. Dividing by \(n-2\) corrects this so \(\text{RSE}^2\) is unbiased for \(\sigma^2\). (Dividing by a smaller number makes the RSE larger, not smaller.)

Explain to a friend the difference between the population regression line and the least-squares line, using Figure 3.3.

The population line \(\beta_0+\beta_1X\) uses the true, unknown coefficients: it is the average relationship in the whole population (in Figure 3.3, the red line \(2+3X\)). The least-squares line uses estimates computed from one sample, so it wobbles from sample to sample (the light-blue lines). Each least-squares line is a bit off, but they are unbiased: on average they equal the population line, just as sample means average to the population mean. Standard errors measure how big the wobble typically is.
