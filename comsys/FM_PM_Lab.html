Part 1

Build the picture

Six small steps. By the end you will know what FM and PM actually do, and why their formulas look the way they do.

1

A wave is a spinning hand

Picture a clock hand spinning round and round.

Now watch only how high its tip is. As the hand spins, the height goes up and down. Draw that height over time and you get a wave.

Three things describe the hand:

  • Length of the hand = amplitude \(A\). This sets how tall the wave is.
  • Total angle turned so far = \(\theta(t)\). This is a running total.
  • How fast it turns = frequency. A carrier turns at a steady speed \(\omega_c\), so \(\theta=\omega_c t\).
Every wave on this page
\[\varphi(t)=A\cos\theta(t)\]
Length \(A\) and angle \(\theta(t)\). That is all a wave is.
Baby version

Modulation means: mess with the spinning hand so that it carries your message. You can only mess with it in two ways. Change its length, or change its angle.

2

Three ways to mess with the hand

Watch one slow message control three hands at once.

FM and PM both change the angle and leave the length alone. That is why Lathi groups them as angle modulation.

The length never changes, so the wave's height never changes. Keep this in mind. It is the reason FM power is so easy (lesson 9).

The car picture (use it for everything)

Think of the hand as a car on a circular track. \(\theta(t)\) is the odometer: total distance driven. The frequency is the speedometer.

  • FM: the message holds the speedometer.
  • PM: the message holds the odometer. It says how far ahead of a ghost car you must be.
3

Frequency is the slope of the angle

If \(\theta(t)\) is the odometer, the speed is how fast the odometer is climbing. "How fast something climbs" is a derivative.

Instantaneous frequency — the one idea behind everything
\[\omega_i(t)=\frac{d\theta}{dt}\ \ \text{(rad/s)}\]\[f_i(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{d\theta}{dt}\ \ \text{(Hz)}\]
Steep angle curve → fast spinning → crowded wave.

Three rules fall out:

  • Plain carrier: \(\theta=\omega_c t\) → \(\omega_i=\omega_c\). Constant.
  • Anything added to \(\omega_c t\) is the modulation \(\psi(t)\): \(\theta=\omega_c t+\psi(t)\) → \(\omega_i=\omega_c+\dot\psi(t)\).
  • A constant added to the angle (like \(+0.3\pi\) or \(+8000\)) differentiates to zero. It changes nothing about frequency.

And backwards: the angle is the running total of the frequency, \(\theta(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{t}\omega_i(\alpha)\,d\alpha\).

Exam lens · this exact skill was tested
  • \(\cos(50t^6+t^4+8000)\) → \(\omega_i=300t^5+4t^3\). At \(t=2\): \(9600+32=\mathbf{9632}\) rad/s. (2025 Quiz 2)
  • \(3.6\cos(6.8t^2+2t)\) → \(\omega_i=13.6t+2\). At \(t=1\): \(\mathbf{15.6}\) rad/s. The 3.6 plays no part. (lab quiz Q25)
4

FM: the message drives the speedometer

In FM we make the speed follow the message directly. Speed goes up by \(k_f\) for every volt of message.

FM rule → FM signal
\[\omega_i=\omega_c+k_f\,m(t)\]\[\Downarrow\]\[\theta(t)=\omega_c t+k_f\!\int_{-\infty}^{t}\! m(\alpha)\,d\alpha\] \[\varphi_{FM}(t)=A\cos\!\Big[\omega_c t+k_f\!\int_{-\infty}^{t}\! m\,d\alpha\Big]\]
The integral is there only because angle = running total of speed (lesson 3).
The three FM numbers
\[\Delta f=\frac{k_f\,m_p}{2\pi}\qquad \beta=\frac{\Delta f}{B}\]\[f_i\ \text{swings between}\ f_c\pm\Delta f\]
\(m_p\) = biggest |m(t)|. \(B\) = highest message frequency. \(\beta\) = "how many message-widths the frequency swings".
Units trap

\(k_f\) is usually in rad/s per volt, so divide by \(2\pi\). But if the formula is written \(2\pi k_f\int m\), or \(k_f\) is given in Hz/V (like "20 kHz/V" in 2026 Q1), then \(\Delta f=k_f m_p\) directly. No \(2\pi\).

5

PM: the message drives the odometer

In PM the message sets how far ahead of the ghost hand you are. Lead \(=k_p\,m(t)\) radians.

Now think like a driver. To get further ahead of the ghost car, you must drive faster than it. Once you hold a fixed lead, you drive at the same speed as it. To fall back, you drive slower.

So in PM the speed depends on how fast the message is changing, its slope \(\dot m\). Not on the message itself.

PM rule → PM frequency
\[\varphi_{PM}(t)=A\cos\!\big[\omega_c t+k_p\,m(t)\big]\]\[\Downarrow\]\[\omega_i=\omega_c+k_p\,\dot m(t)\] \[\Delta f=\frac{k_p\,\dot m_p}{2\pi}\qquad \Delta\phi=k_p\,m_p\]
\(\dot m_p\) = steepest slope of m. \(\Delta\phi\) = peak phase lead.
Say it out loud

FM: frequency looks like m. PM: frequency looks like the slope of m. That single sentence answers half of all sketch questions.

Part 2

The pictures the exam asks for

"Sketch \(\varphi_{FM}\) and \(\varphi_{PM}\)" is a guaranteed question type. Here is the recipe, and why FM and PM are secretly one machine.

7

Triangle and square, FM and PM

The sketch recipe (never changes)
  1. Draw \(f_i(t)\) first. FM: same shape as m. Mark \(f_c\pm k_f m_p/2\pi\). PM: same shape as \(\dot m\).
  2. For PM: ramps → constant frequencies. Flats → \(f_c\). Jumps → phase jump of \(k_p\times\)(jump height).
  3. Draw a constant-height carrier. Crowd the cycles where \(f_i\) is high, spread them where it is low.
  4. At a jump, \(\dot m\) is an impulse and \(f_i\) is undefined. Say so in one line; keys usually ignore it.
Why \(k_p m_p\lt\pi\)

The receiver only sees phase modulo \(2\pi\). If m jumps and \(k_p m\) is allowed outside \((-\pi,\pi)\), two different message values give the same phase. So the message can't be recovered uniquely (Lathi 4.5-2, lab quiz Q27).

8

Same machine, different filter in front

Look at the two formulas again. FM puts \(\int m\) in the angle. PM puts \(m\) in the angle. So:

The swap rule
\[\text{FM of } m \;=\; \text{PM of } \textstyle\int m\]\[\text{PM of } m \;=\; \text{FM of } \dot m\]
Exam lens
  • "FM generated using a phase modulator" means \(\int m\) is fed to the phase modulator. Use \(m_p\), not \(\dot m_p\) (2024 Midsem Q3).
  • Box says "Phase modulator" but an integrator sits before it? It is FM. Calling it PM is the most common mistake on 2026 Q2.
  • You can't tell FM from PM by looking at a wave. A constant in FM and a ramp in PM give the same wave (Lathi 4.5-4).
Part 3

Spectrum, power, bandwidth

Where the energy goes, and how much room on the dial an FM station needs.

9

Power never changes. It only moves.

The hand's length is always \(A\). So the power is always the same as an unmodulated carrier:

Angle-modulated power, any message
\[P=\frac{A^2}{2}\]
"Power of \(10\cos(\omega_c t+0.1\sin2000\pi t)\)" → 50 W. "Power 200 W" → \(A=20\).

Now send a single tone, \(m=a\cos\omega_m t\). For FM, \(k_f\int m=\beta\sin\omega_m t\) with

Tone FM: index and spectrum
\[\beta=\frac{k_f\,a}{\omega_m}=\frac{\Delta f}{f_m}\]\[\varphi_{FM}=A\!\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\!J_n(\beta)\cos(\omega_c+n\omega_m)t\]
Lines at \(f_c+nf_m\), spaced \(f_m\) apart, heights \(A|J_n(\beta)|\). \(J_{-n}=(-1)^nJ_n\).
Baby version

Modulation doesn't add energy. It cuts the same pizza into more slices. Bigger β → more slices → wider spread.

Facts to keep
  • Carrier line keeps \(J_0^2(\beta)\) of the power. Zero at \(\beta=2.405\) (and again at 5.52).
  • \(J_n\) is tiny once \(n>\beta+1\). Lines up to \(n=\beta+1\) hold at least 98% of the power → that is Carson's rule.
  • PM with the same tone: \(\beta=k_p a\), which doesn't depend on \(f_m\). So \(\Delta f=\beta f_m\) grows with \(f_m\).
  • Constant envelope → efficient class-C amplifiers, and FM ignores amplitude noise.
10

Tiny β: narrowband FM is AM with a 90° twist

Let \(a(t)=\int m\,dt\). If \(k_f a(t)\) stays much smaller than 1 rad, use \(\cos x\approx1\) and \(\sin x\approx x\):

NBFM and NBPM
\[\varphi_{NBFM}\approx A\cos\omega_c t-A\,k_f\,a(t)\,\sin\omega_c t\] \[\varphi_{NBPM}\approx A\cos\omega_c t-A\,k_p\,m(t)\,\sin\omega_c t\]
Carrier + a DSB term. So bandwidth \(\approx 2B\), exactly like AM.
Exam lens
  • The sideband rides on \(\sin\omega_c t\) (90° shifted). That is why an envelope detector can't read NBFM.
  • 2026 Q2 had \(\beta=0.00125\) → NBFM, \(B_T\approx2B\).
  • NBFM is how Armstrong makes clean FM: crystal oscillator + 90° shift + one DSB-SC modulator + adder.
11

Carson's rule: how wide is FM?

The frequency swings between \(f_c-\Delta f\) and \(f_c+\Delta f\). That covers \(2\Delta f\). But the swinging itself happens at the message rate, and that smears each edge by about \(B\). So:

Carson's rule
\[B_T\approx2(\Delta f+B)=2B(\beta+1)\]
\(\beta\ll1\) → \(B_T\approx2B\) (NBFM). \(\beta\gg1\) → \(B_T\approx2\Delta f\) (WBFM).
What B means
  • \(B\) = the highest message frequency, never the lowest. Two tones at 100 and 250 Hz → \(B=250\) Hz.
  • Periodic non-sinusoid (triangle, trapezoid): the question tells you which harmonic to use (3rd, 5th, 7th).
  • Two or more tones: the peaks add. \(\Delta\omega\le\) sum of each term's peak. Use the sum.
  • Carson is an estimate. For \(2\lt\beta\lt10\) Lathi notes \(2(\Delta f+2B)\) is safer, but use Carson unless asked.

The PM penalty

FM bandwidth hardly cares about message frequency. PM bandwidth grows with it, because faster messages are steeper. That is why broadcast uses FM.

13

Multipliers multiply. Mixers only move.

A frequency multiplier ×n (a nonlinear device plus a bandpass filter) turns \(\cos\theta\) into \(\cos n\theta\). The whole angle is multiplied: \(f_c\), \(\Delta f\), β and \(\Delta\phi\) all go ×n.

A mixer multiplies by \(\cos\omega_{LO}t\). It adds or subtracts \(\omega_{LO}t\) from the angle. Only \(f_c\) moves. \(\Delta f\) is untouched.

Armstrong recipe (every variant)
  1. Deviation fixes the total: \(n_1n_2=\Delta f_{final}/\Delta f_1\).
  2. Carrier fixes the split: after stage 2, \(f_c=n_2 f_{mix}\), so \(n_2=f_c/f_{mix}\).
  3. Mixer: \(f_{mix}=|n_1f_1\pm f_{LO}|\). Solve for \(f_{LO}\). Check the allowed range.
Part 4

The exam toolkit

Almost every question in this chapter is one of four moves. Find which move it is, then do it.

MOVE 1

Differentiate the angle

Given \(A\cos\theta(t)\)? Drop \(A\), drop constants, differentiate: \(\omega_i=\dot\theta\). Divide by \(2\pi\) for Hz.

MOVE 2

Find peak Δf

FM → biggest value \(m_p\). PM → steepest slope \(\dot m_p\). Several tones → add the peaks.

MOVE 3

β and Carson

\(\beta=\Delta f/B\), \(B_T=2(\Delta f+B)\). B = highest message frequency (or named harmonic).

MOVE 4

Track boxes

×n multiplies \(f_c\), Δf, β. Mixer moves \(f_c\) only. Integrator in front of PM → FM.

Memorise these 6 lines
  1. \(f_i=\frac1{2\pi}\frac{d\theta}{dt}\). FM: \(f_i=f_c+\frac{k_f}{2\pi}m\). PM: \(f_i=f_c+\frac{k_p}{2\pi}\dot m\).
  2. \(\Delta f_{FM}=\frac{k_fm_p}{2\pi}\), \(\Delta f_{PM}=\frac{k_p\dot m_p}{2\pi}\), \(\beta=\Delta f/B\), \(\Delta\phi_{PM}=k_pm_p\).
  3. Carson \(B_T=2(\Delta f+B)\). NBFM ≈ \(2B\). WBFM ≈ \(2\Delta f\).
  4. Power always \(A^2/2\). Tone lines \(AJ_n(\beta)\) at \(f_c+nf_m\). Carrier vanishes at β = 2.405.
  5. ×n multiplies \(f_c\), Δf, β. Mixer moves \(f_c\) only. \(f_c\) changing alone leaves Δf alone.
  6. PM of m = FM of ṁ. FM of m = PM of ∫m. PM needs \(k_pm_p\lt\pi\) if m jumps.

Power and bandwidth of AM, FM, and AM riding on FM

\(A_c=10\) V, \(f_c=100\) MHz, \(m_1=\cos2\pi f_1t\) with \(f_1=2\) kHz, \(m_2=\cos2\pi f_2t\) with \(f_2=10\) kHz, \(k_a=0.5\), \(k_f=20\) kHz/V.
Find the power (1 Ω) and bandwidth of:
(1) \(s_1=A_c[1+k_am_1]\cos2\pi f_ct\)
(2) \(s_2=A_c\cos(2\pi f_ct+2\pi k_f\!\int\! m_2)\)
(3) \(s_3=A_c[1+k_am_1]\,\times\)
\(\quad\cos(2\pi f_ct+2\pi k_f\!\int\! m_2)\)

Picture it: power lives in the hand's length, never its angle. For \(s_3\), every FM line grows its own little AM sidebands.

Hint

\(k_f\) is in Hz/V and the phase is written \(2\pi k_f\int m\), so \(\Delta f=k_f\max|m_2|\) directly. Multiplying two signals adds their bandwidths.

Find

Power \(P\) (1 Ω) and bandwidth \(B\) of \(s_1\) (AM), \(s_2\) (FM) and \(s_3\) (AM riding on FM).

Understand it fully · power is the square of the "length", bandwidth is how far the lines spread

Every signal here is "something × cos(angle)". The something in front (the envelope) decides the power. The angle decides where the frequencies sit (the bandwidth). Keep those two jobs separate and this question becomes easy.

Power of one cosine. \(A\cos(\text{anything})\) swings between −A and +A. Its square averages to \(A^2/2\) because \(\cos^2\) averages to ½. So a 10 V carrier carries \(10^2/2=50\) W into 1 Ω, no matter how its angle wiggles.

Power of several cosines at different frequencies: just add each one's \(A^2/2\). Cross terms average to zero.

AM (\(s_1\)): the envelope \(A_c[1+\mu\cos\omega_1t]\) breathes in and out. Expanding with \(\cos a\cos b=\frac12[\cos(a-b)+\cos(a+b)]\) gives three cosines: the carrier (amplitude \(A_c\)) and two sidebands (amplitude \(\mu A_c/2\) each) at \(f_c\pm f_1\). Three lines, spread from \(f_c-f_1\) to \(f_c+f_1\): bandwidth \(2f_1\).

FM (\(s_2\)): the amplitude never changes, only the angle. So power = \(A_c^2/2\) exactly. The frequency swings by \(\Delta f\) either side of \(f_c\), and Carson's rule says the spectrum needs about \(2(\Delta f+f_m)\).

AM on FM (\(s_3\)): it's the FM signal with a breathing envelope. Power comes from the envelope only (same as AM). For the bandwidth: multiplying by \(\cos\omega_1t\) copies the whole FM spectrum to ±2 kHz, so every FM line grows two little AM sidebands, and the band gets 2 kHz wider on each side.
