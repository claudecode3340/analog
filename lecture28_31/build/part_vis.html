6.0 Picture first: what Lectures 34–37 are really doing

Up to Lecture 33 we timed one gate. From Lecture 34 on, the question changes to: a signal has to pass through several gates in a row. How big should each gate be so that the whole trip is as fast as possible? Everything after that is a few simple ideas, shown here as pictures before any formula.

Idea 1 · One gate = a fixed overhead + the work of charging what it drives

Relay-race picture A path is a relay race. Each runner (gate) takes $d_i = f_i + p_i$. The total time is the sum. You can make a runner bigger (stronger), but then the runner before it has to push a heavier load. Sizing is about sharing the work fairly.

Idea 2 · Why we multiply the efforts: the middle sizes cancel

Idea 3 · If the product is fixed, the sum is smallest when all parts are equal

Say it out loud "Product fixed, sum smallest when equal." That one sentence is the whole reason for $\hat f = F^{1/N}$.

Idea 4 · Branching: current that leaks to a side branch

Every Lecture 34–35 path question in 5 moves
  1. Under each gate write $g$ and $p$ (table in 5.2).
  2. Multiply: $F = G\cdot B\cdot H$ (idea 2 + idea 4).
  3. Share fairly: $\hat f = F^{1/N}$ (idea 3), $\hat D = N\hat f + P$.
  4. Walk backwards from the load: $C_{in} = g\,C_{out}/\hat f$ (Fig. 6.5 shows it step by step).
  5. Check that the first gate comes out equal to the given input cap.

8.0 Picture first: Lecture 36 is about changing g and p

Lecture 36 asks "which way of building a function is fastest?" The tools are the same as Lecture 34: every gate is just its $g$ and $p$. A big complex gate saves a stage, but its long series stacks make $g$ and $p$ larger. Bars make the comparison obvious.

For sizing, think of a resistance budget: every path from a rail to Y must add up to exactly $R$ (one unit transistor). How you split the budget between the transistors on a path is up to you, which is the whole idea behind the complex-gate sizing (8.4) and asymmetric gates (8.5).

Try it yourself: pseudo-NMOS strength ratio

Slide the ratio $k_n/k_p$. The VTC (blue) and the low output level $V_{OL}$ update. A stronger NMOS gives a lower $V_{OL}$, but a weak PMOS makes the rising edge slow.

9.0 Picture first: two separate ideas

Skewed gates (9.1) A normal gate is balanced: rising and falling are equally strong. If only one edge matters, shrink the transistors of the other edge. The gate gets a smaller input cap, so the stage before it is faster, and the important edge keeps its full strength. You pay with a slower unimportant edge.
Ratioed gates (9.2–9.3) Replace the whole PMOS network by one always-on pull-up (a resistor, or a PMOS with its gate grounded). Fewer transistors and less input cap, but when the NMOS turns on the two fight. The output low level is a tug-of-war result (not 0 V), and current flows all the time the output is low.
